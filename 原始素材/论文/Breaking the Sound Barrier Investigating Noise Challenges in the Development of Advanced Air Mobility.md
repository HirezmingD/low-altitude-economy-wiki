Advanced air mobility (AAM) is expected to transform urban transportation and is driven by innovations such as electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) vehicles and autonomous drone services (*[1](#bibr1-03611981251393207) [–](#bibr2-03611981251393207) [3](#bibr3-03611981251393207)*). AAM technology has the potential to revolutionize how people move within cities, providing faster, direct connections and alleviating traffic congestion on traditional ground-based transport routes. As urbanization accelerates, the need for efficient and sustainable mobility solutions has never been more critical ( *[4](#bibr4-03611981251393207)* , *[5](#bibr5-03611981251393207)* ).

However, with the advancement of AAM comes a critical challenge that must be addressed to realize its full potential in urban environments—noise pollution (*[6](#bibr6-03611981251393207) [–](#bibr8-03611981251393207) [9](#bibr9-03611981251393207)*). The distinct characteristics of AAM vehicles, including VTOL capabilities and low-altitude flight operations, introduce unique noise-related concerns. The proximity of AAM operations to urban communities raises apprehensions about the potential impacts of noise on residents, businesses, and the overall urban environment ( *[10](#bibr10-03611981251393207)* , *[11](#bibr11-03611981251393207)* ).

Noise pollution in urban areas has been well documented as a significant public health concern, with adverse effects on physical and psychological wellbeing. Chronic exposure to high noise levels has been linked to stress, sleep disturbances, cardiovascular problems, and decreased overall quality of life. Moreover, excessive noise disrupts the tranquility of public spaces, affecting social activities and diminishing the overall urban living experience ( *[12](#bibr12-03611981251393207)* , *[13](#bibr13-03611981251393207)* ).

Navigating the noise challenges in AAM implementation effectively ensures the technology’s long-term acceptance and success in urban environments. Addressing noise pollution is a matter of regulatory compliance and a key factor in gaining public trust and fostering community acceptance. Understanding the impacts of AAM noise on urban communities and deploying effective noise mitigation strategies are paramount to harnessing the true potential of AAM technology for urban mobility ( *[14](#bibr14-03611981251393207)* , *[15](#bibr15-03611981251393207)* ).

Consequently, this research paper aims to comprehensively explore the noise challenges associated with implementing AAM in urban airspace. By examining noise modeling and prediction techniques, noise reduction technologies, airspace management, and community engagement strategies, we seek to identify best practices and lessons learned from real-world AAM projects. Addressing the noise challenges head-on paves the way for sustainable and community-friendly urban airspace, where AAM contributes to a quieter and more efficient urban transportation landscape while advancing AAM technologies.

## Literature Review

## Overview and Definition of an AAM

The introduction of AAM marks a paradigm shift in aviation and surface transportation systems. With rapid urbanization and escalating demand for efficient mobility solutions, the strain on traditional transportation is increasing rapidly ( *[16](#bibr16-03611981251393207)* ). In response to these challenges, AAM emerges as a promising alternative that envisions using electric-powered, autonomous aerial vehicles to transform how people and goods are transported. AAM encompasses a wide range of eVTOL aircraft, autonomous drones, and urban air taxis that promise to address pressing transportation challenges such as minimizing congestion, improving connectivity, and enhancing mobility in metropolitan and regional settings ( *[1](#bibr1-03611981251393207)* , *[2](#bibr2-03611981251393207)* , *[17](#bibr17-03611981251393207)* ).

The core principles of AAM are rooted in sustainability, efficiency, and the integration of cutting-edge technologies to provide direct aerial transportation connectivity within urban environments. By leveraging electric propulsion systems, advanced materials, and autonomous flight capabilities, AAM seeks to offer more environmentally friendly and cost-effective solutions than traditional air travel. In addition, AAM implementation has gained significant momentum in recent years because of advancements in aerospace engineering, battery technologies, artificial intelligence, automation technology, and the increasing demand for faster, safer, and more convenient transportation options ( *[8](#bibr8-03611981251393207)* , *[14](#bibr14-03611981251393207)* ).

The National Aeronautics and Space Administration definition of AAM is “to encompass developing and deploying aviation in transformative and innovative manners to provide aerial mobility in ways not typically seen today” ( *[18](#bibr18-03611981251393207)* ). As such, researchers believe that AAM represents a shift from traditional aviation models, such as commercial airlines or helicopters, toward more efficient, sustainable, and versatile air transportation modes. It is expected to revolutionize various industries, including urban transportation, logistics, and emergency response ( *[19](#bibr19-03611981251393207)* ). Furthermore, the European Union Aviation Safety Agency asserts that an AAM is “a concept of operations for the use of manned and unmanned aerial vehicles in a controlled airspace, which may be segregated or integrated with other air traffic while providing safe, secure, and efficient transportation of passengers and goods” ( *[20](#bibr20-03611981251393207)* ). Similarly, the Federal Aviation Administration states that, “an AAM is a transportation system that moves people and property by air between two points in the United States (U.S.) using aircraft with advanced technologies, including electric aircraft, or electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) aircraft, in both controlled and uncontrolled airspace” ( *[20](#bibr20-03611981251393207)* ).

## Distinction between Advanced Air Mobility, Urban Aircraft Systems, and Unmanned Aerial Vehicles

To contextualize the noise challenges explored in this study, it is essential first to delineate the operational and technological distinctions between unmanned aircraft systems (UAS), unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), and AAM platforms. According to scholars such as Walulik et al. ( *[8](#bibr8-03611981251393207)* ), and Hoopes and Takahashi ( *[21](#bibr21-03611981251393207)* ), UAVs generally denote individually remote-piloted or autonomous aircraft used for surveillance, mapping, or parcel delivery ( *[8](#bibr8-03611981251393207)* ). Cohen et al. ( *[4](#bibr4-03611981251393207)* ) further highlighted that UAS extends this definition to include the comprehensive ecosystem supporting UAV operations, encompassing ground control stations, communication links, and command infrastructure ( *[4](#bibr4-03611981251393207)* , *[22](#bibr22-03611981251393207)* ). In contrast, AAM represents a transformative aviation paradigm characterized by the deployment of piloted or autonomous eVTOL vehicles for on-demand passenger and/or cargo transport within controlled and integrated urban and regional airspace environments ( *[18](#bibr18-03611981251393207)* , *[20](#bibr20-03611981251393207)* , *[21](#bibr21-03611981251393207)* ). Whereas UAVs and UAS typically operate at low altitudes over short ranges in loosely regulated or isolated settings, AAM systems are designed for medium- to long-range missions with stringent requirements for airspace coordination, infrastructure integration, and public interface ( *[3](#bibr3-03611981251393207)* , *[10](#bibr10-03611981251393207)* ). These fundamental disparities result in divergent acoustic signatures, exposure profiles, and community impacts, necessitating differentiated analytical frameworks for noise assessment and mitigation ( *[11](#bibr11-03611981251393207)* ).

Consequently, understanding the nuanced differences between UAVs, UAS, and AAM systems is essential for accurately framing their respective noise characteristics, regulatory contexts, and integration challenges. These distinctions are particularly relevant given that much of the foundational literature on aerial noise has been derived from studies centered on UAV or UAS operations, which differ in scale, propulsion architecture, and airspace usage compared with AAM platforms. [Table 1](#table1-03611981251393207) presents a structured comparison highlighting critical dimensions such as vehicle type, operational altitude, propulsion systems, noise impact profiles, and community interface to support a more transparent analytical framework and avoid conflation across these categories. This comparative overview serves as a reference point for subsequent sections of the study, where the analysis focuses explicitly on noise challenges and mitigation strategies unique to AAM systems within complex urban airspace environments.

## Characteristics of AAM Vehicles and Their Noise Emissions

The critical characteristics of AAM include innovative technologies such as electric or hybrid-electric aerial vehicles, autonomous flight systems, and new ground infrastructures to provide on-demand and point-to-point air mobility services ( *[22](#bibr22-03611981251393207)* ). Consequently, AAM vehicles exhibit distinctive operational features that set them apart from traditional fixed-wing aircraft. These features include distributed propulsion systems, smaller fuselage sizes, and VTOL capabilities, which enable them to operate in confined spaces. However, these unique characteristics contribute to varying noise emissions during various phases of flight ( *[21](#bibr21-03611981251393207)* ).

The VTOL of AAM vehicles involves using multiple rotors or fans, which can generate high-intensity noise during takeoff and landing maneuvers. In addition, AAM vehicles often operate at lower altitudes and speeds than conventional aircraft, resulting in prolonged noise exposure to ground-level observers during low-altitude flight ( *[22](#bibr22-03611981251393207)* ). Notable aviation agencies such as the FAA and the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) assert that understanding these noise sources and their specific characteristics is vital for addressing the challenges associated with AAM noise management in urban environments ( *[20](#bibr20-03611981251393207)* , *[23](#bibr23-03611981251393207)* ). For instance, the United States currently regulates aviation operational noise through the implementation of the 49 U.S. Code (U.S.C.) 44715 to “protect the public health and welfare from aircraft noise” by enforcing noise standards and aircraft testing standards for airworthiness certification ( *[20](#bibr20-03611981251393207)* ).

## AAM Noise Impacts on Urban Communities

Current scholarly works suggest that introducing AAM in urban airspace has sparked public concerns about the potential impacts of noise on residents, businesses, and the overall urban environment (*[25](#bibr25-03611981251393207) [–](#bibr26-03611981251393207) [27](#bibr27-03611981251393207)*). High levels of noise pollution can significantly impact public health and wellbeing, leading to increased stress, sleep disturbances, and decreased cognitive performance. Urban communities, especially those near AAM flight paths and landing zones, may experience disruptions to daily activities, reduced outdoor recreational opportunities, reduced property values, and compromised social interactions because of excessive AAM operational noise levels ( *[11](#bibr11-03611981251393207)* , *24*, *[28](#bibr28-03611981251393207)* ).

According to Kennedy and Buys ( *[29](#bibr29-03611981251393207)* ), the adverse effects of urban noise pollution extend beyond individual wellbeing to affect community cohesion and urban livability. AAM noise may also affect sensitive facilities such as hospitals, schools, and cultural centers, potentially hampering their functionality and ability to provide essential services to residents. As AAM technology progresses, understanding the potential consequences of AAM noise on urban communities becomes imperative for devising effective noise management strategies for sustained operational efficiency and fostering public acceptance ( *[30](#bibr30-03611981251393207)* , *[31](#bibr31-03611981251393207)* ).

## Relevance for AAM Noise Management Implementation

The challenges posed by AAM noise necessitate the development of comprehensive noise management strategies for successful implementation in urban environments. Given the unique characteristics of AAM vehicles and their operations, adapting conventional aircraft noise reduction measures may not be sufficient ( *[19](#bibr19-03611981251393207)* ). Effective noise management entails a holistic approach encompassing noise modeling, route planning, noise zoning, technological innovations, regulatory frameworks, and community engagement ( *[17](#bibr17-03611981251393207)* ).

Noise management in AAM implementation is critical for regulatory compliance, fostering public acceptance, and ensuring the sustainable integration of AAM technology within densely populated urban areas. By proactively addressing noise challenges, AAM stakeholders can establish a foundation for a quieter and more community-friendly urban airspace, enhancing the potential of AAM to revolutionize urban mobility while minimizing its impacts on the wellbeing and quality of life of urban residents.

## AAM Noise Management Planning and Mitigation

While noise mitigation of AAM operations has been touched on in various studies, the primary focus centered on the design of the rotor blades. This focus neglects other potentially effective mitigation strategies, such as material and structural design alterations. Furthermore, although AAM is often the nominal subject, a significant portion of the existing literature draws heavily from UAV-based frameworks, which differ substantially in operational altitude, vehicle scale, and community exposure. As a result, these approaches may fail to adequately capture the complexities unique to AAM systems, particularly in densely populated urban settings. There is thus a need for noise mitigation methodologies specifically tailored to the operational realities and public interface of AAM.

In addition, AAM noise-planning research has primarily centered on modifying flight trajectories, often employing machine learning techniques or basic planning methods as mitigation strategies. However, these studies usually fall short because of inaccurate input data, particularly concerning AAM technical specifications, as indicated in [Table 2](#table2-03611981251393207). An underexplored but crucial aspect of planning lies in the location of the vertiport; its positioning is widely acknowledged as a critical factor, but no research to date provides a method to identify optimal vertiport locations considering minimal noise impact. This represents a significant gap in the current body of knowledge that future research should strive to address.

## Regulatory Challenges for AAM Noise Management

The regulatory landscape for aviation noise management is not fully equipped to address the unique challenges posed by AAM operations. Current standards established by organizations such as the FAA and ICAO were developed for traditional fixed-wing aircraft and rotorcraft, focusing on high-altitude operations in sparsely populated areas ( *[20](#bibr20-03611981251393207)* , *[23](#bibr23-03611981251393207)* ). However, AAM vehicles, including eVTOL aircraft, operate at lower altitudes and often within urban environments, where noise exposure is more direct and impacts densely populated communities. These operational differences introduce regulatory gaps, particularly in accurately assessing and managing noise impacts that are more localized and complex.

Existing noise metrics, such as A-weighted sound levels, are insufficient to accurately represent the diverse noise profiles of AAM vehicles, which encompass a combination of broadband, tonal, and impulsive sounds resulting from distributed propulsion systems. Moreover, static noise measurement protocols, commonly used in traditional aviation, fail to capture the dynamic nature of AAM operations, such as varying flight paths, altitudes, and proximity to noise-sensitive areas like schools and hospitals. While the FAA’s aviation environmental design tool is currently the standard modeling platform for aviation noise exposure, it was primarily designed for conventional fixed-wing and rotorcraft operations. It may not adequately reflect the low-altitude, distributed, and frequently changing acoustic signatures of AAM systems. As such, there is growing recognition within the research and regulatory communities of the need for a next-generation AAM-specific modeling framework that can accurately simulate noise exposure in urban environments. Parallel to modeling concerns, the lack of a unified, globally accepted noise certification metric for AAM further complicates regulatory consistency. Ongoing efforts by the FAA and ICAO to assess appropriate metrics, such as updated versions of the effective perceived noise level (EPNL) or new psychoacoustic-based indices, will be essential for informing certification processes, planning policies, and public communication strategies. These regulatory shortcomings highlight the urgent need for a comprehensive overhaul of both modeling tools and noise standards to accommodate the novel demands of AAM, ensuring the technology’s integration into urban spaces is both sustainable and equitable.

## Point of Departure for the Study

The rapid advancement of AAM technology offers immense potential for transforming urban transportation by introducing mobility services using eVTOL vehicles and autonomous drones. However, implementing AAM in densely populated urban airspace presents a critical challenge: noise pollution. The unique characteristics of AAM vehicles, including their low-altitude flight and proximity to urban communities, have raised concerns about the potential adverse impacts of noise on residents’ wellbeing and the overall urban environment. Accordingly, although AAM holds promising benefits, noise pollution remains a significant barrier to the widespread adoption and acceptance of AAM technology in urban settings. Chronic exposure to high noise levels has been associated with various health issues, including stress, sleep disturbances, and cardiovascular problems. Furthermore, excessive noise disrupts the tranquility of public spaces, affects social activities, and diminishes the overall quality of life for urban dwellers.

Despite these AAM noise concerns, the plethora of industry reports and academic research data resources available on the development and utilization of air mobility systems appears to be limited in research reviewing specific challenges to the advancements and/or utilization of air mobility systems, such as noise. Therefore, this study aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the noise challenges encountered during the implementation of AAM technology in urban environments and to identify effective strategies for addressing these challenges, thereby facilitating the successful and sustainable integration of AAM in urban transportation systems.

## Problem Statement and Research Questions

The problem statement of this research revolves around understanding and addressing the noise challenges in implementing AAM in urban airspace. The paper seeks to explore the following key research questions and associated objectives:

RQ1: What are the specific noise emissions and sources associated with AAM vehicles during takeoff, landing, and low-altitude flight, and how do they differ from traditional aircraft?

To examine the noise challenges associated with implementing AAM technology in urban airspace, explicitly focusing on the noise emissions of eVTOL vehicles and other AAM innovations.

RQ2: What advancements in noise reduction technologies for AAM vehicles are available, and how can these technologies be optimized to achieve quieter and more environmentally friendly urban AAM operations?

To explore advancements in noise reduction technologies for AAM vehicles and their implications for urban AAM implementation to achieve quieter and more environmentally friendly operations.

To investigate strategies for minimizing noise exposure in urban areas through efficient route planning and noise zoning, considering the need to balance transportation efficiency with community acceptance.

## Literature Search Strategy

The literature search was conducted across Scopus, Web of Science, and Dimensions databases using a structured set of primary and secondary keywords. The goal was to capture a broad yet focused body of research relevant to AAM noise, urban air mobility (UAM) operations, UAV acoustic impacts, and mitigation strategies. The primary keywords used included: “Advanced Air Mobility (AAM),”“Urban Air Mobility (UAM),”“Electric Vertical Take-Off and Landing (eVTOL),”“Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs),”“Unmanned Aircraft Systems (UAS),”“aerial noise,”“aircraft noise,” and “aerial vehicle propulsion.” Secondary keywords were designed to narrow the thematic focus and included: “noise modeling,”“noise mitigation,”“noise perception,”“noise zoning,”“public acceptance,”“AAM regulations,”“UAV acoustics,”“community impact,”“vertiport planning,” and “machine learning for noise prediction.” Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) were applied to develop combinations such as: “Advanced Air Mobility” AND (“noise modeling” OR “noise mitigation”) and “Unmanned Aerial Vehicles” AND (“community acceptance” OR “urban planning”). Keywords unrelated to noise emissions, airspace integration, or acoustic community impacts (e.g., payload delivery, drone surveillance) were excluded to maintain alignment with the research objectives. This systematic approach yielded an initial pool of 522 publications.

## Screening and Inclusion Criteria

The screening process involved three distinct phases to ensure the inclusion of high-quality and relevant studies. The first phase focused on title and abstract screening, during which studies unrelated to noise challenges in AAM or UAVs were excluded. In the second phase, the relevant studies’ full texts were reviewed for methodological rigor and direct relevance to the research questions. The final phase resulted in the selection of 343 publications, which were further categorized into key focus areas, including noise mitigation, regulatory challenges, and urban planning.

## Data Extraction and Organization

To extract meaningful insights from the selected publications, a standardized data extraction form was employed. Each study was evaluated for its title, authorship, publication year, methodological approach, focus area, key findings, and relevance to the research questions.

The selected 343 publications were systematically categorized by their focus areas to facilitate a targeted analysis of AAM noise challenges. [Table 3](#table3-03611981251393207) presents a snapshot of the categorization process, highlighting six primary focus areas. The most significant categorization proportion, with 112 studies, was dedicated to UAS noise mitigation, and another category of 40 studies focused on comparative analysis of UAS and AAM technologies. In addition, [Table 4](#table4-03611981251393207) provides a snapshot of the summary and a clear framework for linking the reviewed studies to the research questions, ensuring that the findings are well supported and systematically derived. These studies offer invaluable foundational insights into noise characteristics, mitigation strategies, and regulatory frameworks. However, while UAS noise mitigation studies provide foundational insights, their applicability to AAM should be interpreted cautiously and contextually, given differences in vehicle design, operational altitude, and intended urban integration.

Research Question 1: What are the specific noise emissions and sources associated with AAM vehicles during takeoff, landing, and low-altitude flight, and how do they differ from traditional aircraft?

## AAM Noise Sources

Different researchers suggest varying sources and classifications for noise emanating from AAM operations. However, the specific noise sources identified by NASA and the Los Angeles Department of Transportation for AAM vehicles that seem to cut across available research during takeoff, landing, and low-altitude flight include the following.

## Rotors and Propellers

The rotating blades of eVTOL vehicles and other AAM aircraft generate significant noise during takeoff and landing, primarily because of the aerodynamic forces acting on the blades.

## Electric Motors

VTOL vehicles, which often utilize electric propulsion systems, can produce noise from their electric motors during operation.

## Aerodynamic Noise

The interaction between the aircraft’s surfaces and the surrounding air generates aerodynamic noise during all phases of flight.

## Boundary Layer Noise

The thin boundary layer of air around the aircraft’s surfaces can become turbulent, leading to boundary layer noise, particularly during takeoff and landing.

## Powertrain and Systems Noise

Noise emissions can arise from various powertrain components and mechanical systems onboard AAM vehicles, including batteries, generators, inverters, landing gear mechanisms, control surfaces, and other auxiliary systems.

## Ground Operations

AAM vehicles can produce noise during ground operations, such as taxiing, which can be relevant in urban environments with noise-sensitive areas nearby.

## Blade Slap Noise

Blade slap noise is produced by the interaction between the rotor or propeller blades and the surrounding air during takeoff, landing, and low-altitude flight. The abrupt changes in airflow around the blades can result in distinctive slapping sounds.

## Broadband Noise

Broadband noise covers a wide range of frequencies and is typically generated by the turbulent airflow around the aircraft’s surfaces during all phases of flight.

## Tonal Noise

Tonal noise is a noise component characterized by a distinct pitch or tone, often caused by specific mechanical components or aerodynamic phenomena.

## Impulsive Noise

Impulsive noise is characterized by sudden and sharp noise events, often associated with specific flight maneuvers or changes in engine power settings.

## High-Speed Noise

Some AAM vehicles, particularly those designed for high speeds or supersonic flight, can generate high-speed noise, which includes shockwaves and other aerodynamic effects when flying at or above the speed of sound.

## Ground Noise

During takeoff and landing, AAM vehicles generate noise that is reflected off the ground, which can increase noise levels in the vicinity of the airport or heliport.

## Powertrain Noise

When electric or hybrid AAM vehicles operate, noise emissions can arise from the electric motors, power electronics, and other powertrain components.

## Blade Vortex Interaction Noise

Blade vortex interaction noise occurs when the rotor blades interact with the vortices shed from the previous blade passage, leading to distinctive noise patterns.

## Comparing AAM and Conventional Aircraft Noise Emissions

Based on the typical characteristics of AAM vehicle types compared with traditional aircraft, there is a difference between AAM noise emission and conventional aircraft noise.

## Propulsion Systems

One key difference between AAM vehicles, such as eVTOL aircraft, and traditional aircraft is the propulsion system. AAM vehicles often utilize electric motors, which result in different noise characteristics compared with the jet engines or turboprop engines commonly found in traditional aircraft. Electric propulsion potentially leads to quieter noise profiles during certain flight phases, but again, AAM is expected to have lower flight profiles.

## Flight Profiles

AAM vehicles are designed for short-distance, point-to-point flights within urban areas, often with eVTOL capabilities. This means they are anticipated to operate at lower altitudes and fly overpopulated areas, making noise reduction a critical consideration to ensure community acceptance. On the other hand, traditional aircraft operate over longer distances and typically fly at higher altitudes, potentially reducing their noise impact on the ground.

## Blade and Rotor Design

AAM vehicles, especially those with rotor-based propulsion, exhibit unique noise patterns because of blade designs and interactions with the surrounding air.

## Aerodynamics

AAM vehicles, especially eVTOLs, have unconventional designs to enable vertical takeoff and landing, which can influence their aerodynamic noise characteristics compared with more streamlined traditional aircraft.

Research Question 2: What advancements in noise reduction technologies for AAM vehicles are available, and how can these technologies be optimized to achieve quieter and more environmentally friendly urban AAM operations?

## Advancements in AAM Noise Reduction Technology

Research organizations, including NASA and other industry stakeholders, are actively collaborating on AAM noise reduction technology to address the noise challenges associated with AAM vehicles, particularly eVTOL aircraft. Some of the notable advancements include the following.

## Electric Propulsion

Electric propulsion systems can potentially reduce noise emissions compared with traditional combustion engines. Electric motors offer smoother and quieter operation, especially during takeoff and landing, critical flight phases for noise-sensitive urban areas.

## Distributed Electric Propulsion

Distributed electric propulsion (DEP) systems involve multiple smaller propulsion units distributed along an aircraft’s wings or rotors. This configuration can reduce noise levels, as the noise sources are spread out, and the power demand on each unit is lower, resulting in quieter operation.

## Active Noise Control Systems

Active noise control (ANC) technology involves the use of sensors and actuators to counteract noise by emitting antinoise that cancels out unwanted noise. When integrated into AAM vehicles, ANC technology can offset operational noise, reducing overall noise emissions during specific flight phases.

## Advanced Aerodynamics

Research is being conducted to optimize the aerodynamic design of AAM vehicles to reduce airframe noise during all flight phases. Smooth and streamlined surfaces are necessary to minimize turbulent airflow and reduce noise generation.

## Improved Propulsion Systems

Advancements in electric propulsion systems and DEP lead to quieter operations during takeoff and landing. Quieter electric motors and the distribution of multiple propulsion units help reduce noise levels. In addition, improvements in blade and rotor design being explored in the aerospace industry have the potential to minimize noise generation during rotorcraft operation. Advanced optimized blade/rotor design materials and shape help reduce blade slap noise and improve aerodynamics, resulting in quieter flight.

## Discussion of Findings

This research comprehensively examined the noise challenges and noise reduction technologies associated with AAM operations. A key finding is that AAM noise sources can vary significantly depending on several factors, including the vehicle’s design, size, propulsion type, flight profile, and the surrounding environment. Understanding these diverse noise sources is essential for developing effective noise reduction strategies and designing AAM vehicles that are more environmentally and community-friendly, particularly in urban airspace.

The study also highlights that different types of AAM noise emissions can have varying impacts on surrounding environments and communities. This is especially critical in urban areas, where noise sensitivity is higher because of the dense population and existing high noise levels. The research suggests that engineers and researchers can reduce these noise types through advancements in AAM vehicle design, materials, and propulsion systems. Consequently, recent empirical studies have begun to deepen our understanding of AAM noise impacts and modeling. NASA’s VANGARD project ( *[47](#bibr47-03611981251393207)* ) used psychoacoustic testing to measure annoyance levels from AAM aircraft, revealing that ambient noise context and aircraft phase (takeoff, cruise, landing) significantly influence the public. In parallel, Eve Air Mobility and the Netherlands Aerospace Centre ( *[48](#bibr48-03611981251393207)* ) conducted virtual reality-based experiments in New York City and Orlando to assess how residents perceive eVTOL noise in high versus low background sound environments. On the modeling front, recent machine learning–based frameworks, such as reduced-order semiempirical models integrating ANN for propeller tonal noise prediction ( *[49](#bibr49-03611981251393207)* , *[50](#bibr50-03611981251393207)* ), and urban noise simulations using AI-augmented GIS techniques, demonstrate the growing efficacy of data-driven approaches for AAM noise assessment.

Furthermore, the study notes that significant advancements in AAM noise reduction technology have been made over the past decade, particularly through the efforts of research organizations such as NASA and other industry stakeholders. These researches continuously evolve, aiming to develop innovative solutions supporting quieter and more environmentally friendly AAM operations. Such advancements are critical for the sustainable integration of AAM technology into urban airspace.

In addition to technological advancements, the study underscores the importance of evolving AAM noise mitigation methods in both operational and policy/regulatory planning aspects. Effective noise planning is critical to fostering community acceptance of AAM applications. The research identifies integrative urban AAM noise planning as a critical factor in ensuring that AAM operations can be harmoniously integrated into urban environments by promoting broader acceptance and facilitating the growth of AAM technology. This form of planning is also vital for ensuring that the benefits of AAM technology are realized without compromising the quality of life in urban environments.

While this study draws on several prior investigations centered on UAV and UAS platforms, it is important to recognize that their operational characteristics differ significantly from those of AAM systems. UAVs typically operate at lower altitudes, over shorter distances, and in less densely populated environments, whereas AAM vehicles are intended for passenger and cargo transport through highly regulated urban corridors. Consequently, while UAS/UAV literature offers valuable foundational insights into rotor acoustics and noise propagation, its application to AAM must be treated with caution. Future AAM research must be grounded in context-specific frameworks that reflect the increased vehicle scale, complex flight dynamics, and higher community exposure inherent in urban AAM deployments.

This study demonstrates several strengths in its methodological approach to understanding AAM operational noise challenges. One of the primary strengths of the study is its comprehensive search strategy. The SLR was conducted using a thorough search across three major databases: Scopus, Web of Science, and Dimensions. By utilizing specific keywords and Boolean operators, this study was able to identify a broad and relevant set of studies on noise challenges in AAM. This approach ensured that the review captured a wide range of relevant literature.

Another strength is the rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria applied during the review process. The study employed three phases of screening and exclusion, which helped maintain the integrity and relevance of the synthesized findings. Only high-quality and pertinent studies that fit within the study’s thematic scope and selected timeline were included, ensuring that the review was both focused and comprehensive.

The study also benefits from its systematic data extraction and synthesis methods. By using standardized data extraction forms across the three databases and applying systematic synthesis methods, including both qualitative and quantitative analyses, the study provided a robust framework for identifying key themes and patterns in the literature. This systematic approach strengthened the reliability and depth of the findings.

This tool allowed the researchers to critically evaluate the quality of the included studies, ensuring that the findings were based on reliable and unbiased evidence. This added a layer of rigor to the review, enhancing the trustworthiness of the conclusions drawn.

However, the study also encountered some limitations. One notable limitation is the variability in noise assessment methods used across the included studies. The different techniques and metrics for assessing noise made direct comparisons between studies challenging, which made achieving consistency and generalizability of the findings difficult.

Language and accessibility constraints across databases are another limitation of the study. In addition, access restrictions such as paywalls or proprietary limitations may have prevented the inclusion of some studies, potentially restricting the scope of the review.

Furthermore, the evolving nature of AAM technology presents a limitation to current studies on the subject. Since AAM is a rapidly advancing field, new technologies and findings are continuously emerging. However, a key benefit of this study is its focus on the evolving nature of AAM technology, which positions the findings as a foundation for future research. By addressing the current state of knowledge, the study provides a critical baseline that can be built on as new technologies and innovations in noise mitigation emerge. This forward-looking approach ensures that the study remains relevant, offering insights that will guide ongoing and future advancements in AAM, even as the field continues to develop rapidly beyond the publication period.

In addition, because of the nascent state of AAM development, much of the existing literature remains predominantly conceptual or prototypical, with limited real-world validation. Lastly, while UAS contribute valuable baseline knowledge, particularly in rotor acoustics and early stage noise modeling, their transferability to AAM platforms may be constrained by fundamental differences in system architecture, operational scale, operational corridors, and public exposure. AAM systems, by design, integrate into more regulated airspace, carry passengers or cargo over densely populated zones, and interact directly with urban infrastructure, thereby necessitating bespoke noise analysis frameworks that go beyond what UAS-based research typically covers. Furthermore, while UAS research provides a valuable foundation, particularly in understanding noise sources and initial mitigation strategies, the fundamental differences between UAS and AAM require a tailored approach to particularly suit AAM operational scale, noise generation mechanisms, and the demographic and environmental contexts of operations. However, the significant progress made in UAS integration and community acceptance offers a rich repository of lessons and achievements that can be leveraged for AAM implementation.
