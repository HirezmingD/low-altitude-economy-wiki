## 1\. Introduction

The global low-altitude economy is rapidly transforming airspace utilization patterns at an exponential pace, emerging as a strategic driver for nations vying to lead future industries. Fueled by technological advancements and growing market demand, low-altitude vehicles such as drones and electric vertical take-off and landing (eVTOL) aircraft have moved beyond traditional aviation boundaries, finding widespread use in logistics delivery, medical emergencies, agricultural protection, and beyond. Pioneering efforts—such as the United States Federal Aviation Administration's Urban Air Mobility (UAM) program, the European Union's U-space unmanned aircraft service framework, and China's development of low-altitude economic demonstration zones, demonstrate how major global economies are leveraging institutional innovation to accelerate the market-driven allocation of airspace resources (; ). However, this rapid industrial expansion starkly contrasts with the slow evolution of governance frameworks. The traditional rigid management model, centered on military aviation control, is ill-equipped to accommodate the dynamic and fragmented nature of low-altitude operations (; ). Incidents such as drone disruptions at London Heathrow Airport which caused delays for hundreds of flights, and privacy concerns stemming from unauthorized drone activity in Seoul underscore deep-seated contradictions in multilateral governance. As the global low-altitude vehicle fleet is projected to surpass 1.92 million units by 2028, creating an adaptive equilibrium between technological progress and public safety, while balancing commercial objectives with societal benefits, has emerged as the defining challenge for sustainable sector development.

The essence of airspace governance failure lies in the institutional logic's inability to adapt to the evolving dynamics of resource scarcity. Low-altitude airspace, as a non-renewable and excludable public resource, is characterized by a structural contradiction between its fixed physical capacity and the exponential growth in aircraft numbers driven by the digital age (). This multidimensional scarcity manifests not merely in absolute spatial limitations but more critically in the sophisticated utilization patterns demanded by next-generation aircraft, creating a governance predicament when conventional vertical stratification models confront contemporary operational realities marked by high-density traffic, minimal altitude operations, and polycentric stakeholders (). The resulting structural misalignment generates fundamental tensions as governments oscillate between their paradoxical roles as innovation facilitators and safety guarantors (), enterprises navigate the compliance-innovation dilemma between prohibitive certification requirements and legal risks of regulatory arbitrage (), while the public's asymmetric risk awareness and inadequate engagement mechanisms render them simultaneously incapable of effective oversight yet prone to organized opposition during crises (; ). These intersecting institutional conflicts create a path-dependent governance lag that transforms airspace governance from technical allocation challenges into complex socio-technical crises encompassing innovation ecosystems, risk governance, and social license to operate, ultimately revealing the institutional sclerosis plaguing regulatory systems in the face of rapid technological disruption.

Contemporary scholarship has primarily concentrated on national policymaking and technical standardization, while largely neglecting the inherent interaction dynamics among the tripartite governance actors—government, enterprise, and public—within the context of low-altitude airspace governance. This research bridges this critical gap by developing an innovative analytical approach that synthesizes institutional logic theory with a three-party evolutionary game model, enabling systematic examination of governance challenges and collaborative solutions in low-altitude airspace management. Theoretically, this research constructs an analytical framework that synthesizes institutional logic and behavioral strategies, incorporating the behavioral rationales of government, enterprises, and the public into a unified perspective. This approach reveals the complex interaction mechanisms among multiple stakeholders against the backdrop of low-altitude economic development, bridging the theoretical tension between macro-level institutional structures and micro-level individual behaviors. Not only does this expand the application boundaries of institutional logic theory, but it also provides a universal theoretical model for studying institutional adaptability amid technological transformation. Practically, this research employs dynamic game simulations to analyze the strategic choices and evolutionary trajectories of different stakeholders, identifying key factors and collaborative leverage points that influence governance effectiveness. The findings offer scientific support for policy optimization, industrial synergy, technological innovation, and the establishment of public participation mechanisms, thereby facilitating high-quality and sustainable development of the low-altitude economy.

## 2\. Literature review

### 2.1. Institutional logics theory

Institutional logic, as a pivotal theoretical concept in sociological research, is defined as “a socially constructed and historically developed pattern composed of material practices, cognitive frameworks, values, belief systems, and normative rules that collectively shape the perceptions and behaviors of social actors ()”. At its foundation, it constitutes a set of implicit assumptions and values that influence how organizations perceive reality, evaluate the appropriateness of actions, and conceptualize pathways to success (). In contrast to traditional institutional theory's emphasis on a single dominant logic, institutional logics theory advances a pluralistic framework, positing that contemporary societies are structured by multiple, coexisting logics, including those of the market, profession, corporation, family, community, religion, and state (; ). These logics are not only sources of tension and conflict but also inherently interdependent and co-constitutive (). Their complex interplay generates the institutional forces that guide organizational and individual behavior. Central to this theoretical approach is its focus on the contested, nonlinear nature of institutional change and the resulting diversity of organizational fields and practices (; ). Current scholarship on institutional logics theory develops along two analytical dimensions. Field-level research examines the formative mechanisms and evolutionary trajectories of institutional structures, exploring phenomena such as the institutionalization of carbon neutrality policies (), the establishment of trust frameworks for digital technologies (), and the alignment challenges in green investment paradigms (). Concurrently, organizational-level investigations focus on strategic adaptation patterns when multiple institutional logics compete, analyzing diverse manifestations including the dynamics of elastic hybridity in organizational forms (), the evolution of ethical decision-making in family businesses (), and the institutional tensions inherent in corporate social responsibility implementation ().

### 2.2. Low-altitude economy

Currently, research on the low-altitude economy remains relatively limited. However, as an emerging economic form, its potential value has attracted widespread attention from academia. The novel low-altitude flight activities and related social phenomena derived from it have become hot topics of discussion in both academic and society. Nevertheless, due to the lack of a unified academic consensus on the concept of the low-altitude economy, its connotations, extension, and defining characteristics remain unclear, leading to a diversity of perspectives in related discussions. Despite this, such diversity reflects scholars' multidimensional understanding and interpretation of the low-altitude economy, and provides a solid foundation for the present research.

At the conceptual level, academic understanding of the low-altitude economy exhibits significant diversity. Existing research primarily explores the topic systematically along three core dimensions: Firstly, the technological carrier dimension focuses on analyzing the key technological systems that underpin the development of the low-altitude economy (). Related studies often extend from concepts such as general aviation () and the drone economy (), emphasizing the foundational role of core elements like aircraft technology and airspace management technologies (). Secondly, the transportation system dimension centers on the organizational structure and governance models of low-altitude traffic operations (). It mainly explores topics such as low-altitude traffic management () and urban air mobility (), with particular attention to the optimal allocation of airspace resources and the intelligent upgrading of transportation systems (). Thirdly, the application scenario dimension explores the industrialization pathways of the low-altitude economy. It primarily examines specific fields such as low-altitude tourism () and low-altitude logistics (), aiming to explore business model innovation and the realization of social value ().

Through a systematic review of the aforementioned research findings, this research preliminarily defines the low-altitude economy as a modern economic form that emerges through the deep integration and collaborative innovation of multiple industries, guided by advanced aircraft technologies and utilizing low-altitude airspace as a strategic operational space. Its essential characteristics can be summarized into three key aspects: (a) three-dimensional development of spatial resources, breaking through the spatial constraints of traditional two-dimensional economic activities (Al; ); (b) integrated application of technological innovations, manifested in the convergence and synergy of multiple technological fields such as aviation technology, digital technology, and energy technology (); (c) network-based creation of industrial value, forming a value innovation ecosystem characterized by coordination along the supply chain and cross-sectoral collaboration (; ), as illustrated in. Particularly noteworthy is that the development of the low-altitude economy highly depends on the synchronous advancement of technological innovation and institutional assurance (; Al ). On one hand, breakthroughs in aircraft technology and the development of new equipment form its technological foundation (); on the other hand, intelligent airspace management systems and systematic infrastructure networks provide operational safeguards (; ). This dual-driven mechanism of technology and institutions not only enhances the operational efficiency and safety standards of the low-altitude economy but also continuously unleashes new momentum for economic growth through deep-level industrial integration ().

### 2.3. Low-altitude airspace governance

Airspace generally refers to the atmospheric space above the surface of the Earth where aircraft operate, while low-altitude airspace specifically denotes the space up to a true altitude of 1000 m (; ). With the advancement and widespread adoption of drone technology, low-altitude airspace has become an increasingly significant spatial resource. However, current regulations fail to clearly define the minimum operating altitude for drones, leading to blurred boundaries between airspace usage rights and surface property rights in near-ground airspace. Particularly in urban environments, the widespread application of drones is breaking down the traditional buffer mechanisms between airspace rights and ground rights established. Consequently, the growing number of drones in urban low-altitude areas has made airspace governance more complex ().

Current academic research on low-altitude airspace governance primarily revolves around three core issues: (a) legal definition of low-altitude airspace rights. Due to its spatial overlap characteristics with urban built-up areas, low-altitude airspace interacts complexly with urban spatial rights systems. Existing research mainly adopts a jurisprudential perspective, focusing on the legal considerations related to aviation safety (), regulations for commercial drone use (), and system supervision of vertical airport design (). (b) innovative applications of low-altitude airspace. The international community has developed diverse approaches to urban low-altitude airspace utilization. For example, Japan Airlines pioneered drone delivery trials in mountainous and coastal regions, specifically addressing logistical challenges for disaster relief supplies, daily necessities, and medical deliveries in remote areas (; ); Western countries in Europe and America are actively advancing the construction of UAM systems (), including passenger service tests conducted by Germany's Volocopter in Dubai and America's Joby Aviation in Los Angeles (). (c) systematic planning of low-altitude airspace. Studies in this field encompass three key dimensions: First, capacity assessment research, which involves establishing scientific measurement models to determine the carrying capacity of airspace—this serves as the foundation for optimizing airspace resource allocation (). Second, traffic flow management research, which differs from the static indicator control of land management by emphasizing dynamic traffic flow regulation (; ). Finally, network planning research, focusing primarily on route network design and the optimization of takeoff and landing point layouts (; ).

### 2.4. Research gaps

This research systematically reviews the existing literature and identifies the following critical gaps that remain to be addressed in airspace governance research under the context of low-altitude economy: (a) lack of a dynamic evolution perspective: most existing studies rely on static models or case analysis methods, making it difficult to effectively capture the dynamic characteristics of multi-agent strategies evolving over time in airspace governance. This methodological limitation hinders a deeper understanding of behavioral changes among different stakeholders and their interaction mechanisms across various development stages. (b) limitations of a single-stakeholder perspective: current research on airspace governance mostly focuses on the behavioral analysis of a single stakeholder, while lacking a systematic examination of the complex interactions among the government, enterprises, and the public. This limitation restricts a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms for interest coordination and conflict resolution among multiple stakeholders. (c) insufficient application of the institutional logics theory: although institutional logics theory has matured in organizational behavior and public policy research, its applicability in the governance of low-altitude economy airspace remains underexplored. In particular, further theoretical development and empirical validation are needed to understand how multiple institutional logics influence and shape the strategic choices of different stakeholders. (d) insufficient adaptability of traditional airspace governance frameworks: current airspace governance frameworks fail to adequately address the unique challenges and opportunities brought by the development of the low-altitude economy. There is a particular lack of theoretical constructs specifically addressing the complexities of low-altitude airspace governance, highlighting an urgent need for more targeted theoretical innovation and practical guidance.

## 3\. Methodology

### 3.1. Problem description

The governance of low-altitude airspace is shaped by multiple coexisting institutional logics that collectively define the behavioral objectives and constraints of government agencies, enterprises, and the public, as illustrated in. While the dominant logics guiding these actors may conflict, opportunities for coordination exist. Their behaviors emerge from balancing and responding to competing institutional demands under specific conditions.

Neither the government, enterprises, nor the public operate with perfect rationality due to bounded cognitive capacities, their capacity to acquire, process information, and make predictions is inherently limited. Instead, they learn from the outcomes of others' actions, compare personal gains, and monitor changes in the institutional environment, gradually adjusting their strategies accordingly. They exhibit a tendency to emulate strategies that demonstrate either superior utility or stronger legitimacy within their respective groups. Over time, this dynamic may lead the system toward an equilibrium where unilateral strategy changes offer no advantage.
- (1)
	The government: the state logic

As the custodian of public interests, the government operates within a framework of national sovereignty. Anchored in its institutional commitment to safety-first principles and equitable resource distribution, it seeks to balance three imperatives: safeguarding airspace sovereignty, fostering low-altitude economic growth, and ensuring social equity. Its regulatory system construction represents a materialization of state logic.
- (2)
	Enterprises: the market logic

The primary enterprise actors consist largely of entities operating within the low-altitude economy, such as drone logistics platforms, eVTOL operators, and general aviation companies. Driven by profit maximization, they pursue dual strategies of regulatory circumvention and compliance innovation, leveraging technological advancement and institutional rent-seeking to exploit airspace resources. Their institutional practices are fundamentally market-logic dominated.
- (3)
	The public: the community logic

The public acts as the carrier of community logic, with behavioral choices mediated by social norms and moral cognition. In dimensions such as perceptions of airspace usage rights, risk assessments of safety concerns, and evaluations of environmental impacts, the public constructs a shared understanding of institutional legitimacy through social networks. Their collective actions frequently act as a critical mediating force in resolving institutional tensions.

Therefore, this research aims to systematically analyze the interaction mechanisms among multiple institutional logics in low-altitude airspace governance by constructing a tripartite evolutionary game model involving the government, enterprises, and the public. Focusing on the dynamic game process characterized by conflict and coordination among state logic (government), market logic (enterprises), and community logic (public), the research explores how these three types of actors, under the complex interplay of intertwined, conflicting, and coordinating institutional logics, gradually evolve toward a stable governance equilibrium through continuous strategic interaction and behavioral adaptation. Building upon this foundation, this research seeks to reveal the behavioral logic of actors under institutional tensions and the evolutionary pathways of governance structures, thereby providing theoretical support for achieving stable operation and institutional optimization of low-altitude airspace governance systems. The research will elucidate the interaction mechanisms among actors dominated by different institutional logics during their strategic decision-making processes, and how such interactions shape the overall landscape of low-altitude airspace governance.

### 3.2. Model assumption

Against the backdrop of the rapid development of the low-altitude economy, low-altitude airspace governance is facing increasingly complex challenges. To address this, this research constructs an evolutionary game model involving the participation of the government, enterprises, and the public, aiming to reveal the interaction mechanisms among multiple stakeholders in low-altitude airspace governance, the evolutionary paths of their strategy choices, and the characteristics of dynamic equilibrium. This research is based on the following core assumptions:
- (1)
	Low-altitude airspace governance is regarded as a complex adaptive system, composed of three types of boundedly rational decision-makers: the government, enterprises, and the public. In the initial stage, these stakeholders cannot accurately predict the globally optimal strategy. Instead, they continuously adjust and optimize their strategic choices based on local information, historical experiences, and observations of other stakeholders' behaviors during ongoing interactions and game processes.
- (2)
	Based on institutional logics theory, this research posits that low-altitude airspace governance is not dominated by a single authority or value orientation but is rather a dynamic process shaped by the interplay and negotiation of multiple institutional logics. In this governance arena, state logic, market logic, and community logic coexist and operate in parallel, each embodying distinct value objectives and behavioral norms, and exerting differentiated influences on the strategic choices of governments, enterprises, and the public. To systematically characterize the relative positions and influence intensities of these three institutional logics within the governance structure, this research introduces a set of standardized weighting coefficients: the dominance coefficient of state logic is $\alpha_{s}$, the dominance coefficient of market logic is $\alpha_{m}$, and the dominance coefficient of community logic is $\alpha_{c}$. These coefficients quantify the discursive power, resource mobilization capacity, and rule-shaping ability of different institutional forces within the overall governance mechanism. Specifically, the state logic coefficient $\alpha_{s}$ reflects the dominant role of the government as a representative of public interests and an institutional supplier, emphasizing airspace safety, public order, regulatory enforcement, and strategic coordination. The market logic coefficient $\alpha_{m}$ reflects the efficiency orientation and economic rationality of enterprises in resource allocation, technological innovation, and service provision. The community logic coefficient $\alpha_{c}$ represents the degree of participation of the public as affected groups in the governance process, encompassing concerns over social issues such as flight safety, noise pollution, privacy protection, and equitable accessibility. Furthermore, this research sets $\alpha_{s} + \alpha_{m} + \alpha_{c} = 1$, normalizing the combined influence of the three types of institutional logics into a comprehensive governance weighting system. This constraint not only ensures the comparability and systematicity of the logic weights but also reflects the finite nature of governance resources and decision-making space.
- (3)
	To characterize the government's flexible choices and behavioral uncertainty in regulatory intensity, this research assumes that the government adopts a strict regulatory strategy with probability $x$ and a lenient regulation strategy with probability $1−x$. When the government opts for a strict regulatory strategy, the regulatory intensity is $r_{1}$. In this case, the government tends to strengthen law enforcement, raise compliance standards, increase penalties for violations, and promote comprehensive oversight across the entire chain. It may also enhance governance effectiveness through measures such as frequent inspections, real-time monitoring of flight data, and a blacklist system. When the government adopts a lenient regulation strategy, the regulatory intensity decreases to $r_{2} \left(r_{1} > r_{2}\right)$. Under this model, the government places greater emphasis on stimulating market vitality and technological innovation, allowing enterprises more room for error and greater developmental freedom. However, without effective constraints, this approach may also lead some enterprises to exploit regulatory loopholes, foster opportunistic behavior, and thereby accumulate systemic safety risks.
- (4)
	Enterprises, as the primary actors conducting low-altitude flight activities, must balance compliance costs and potential risks while pursuing maximum economic benefits. To model this, this research sets the following: enterprises adopt a high-investment compliance strategy with probability $y$, and an opportunistic compliance strategy with probability $1−y$. When an enterprise chooses the high-investment compliance strategy, it strictly adheres to national regulations and industry standards throughout its entire operational chain, and proactively accepts government supervision and public oversight. Conversely, when an enterprise opts for the opportunistic compliance strategy, it selectively implements compliance requirements or finds ways to circumvent them. If the government implements a strict regulatory strategy, it will simultaneously establish incentive and penalty mechanisms: providing a subsidy $S$ to high-investment compliant enterprises as a reward, and imposing a fine $F$ on opportunistic compliant enterprises as a penalty.
- (5)
	To characterize the public's behavioral choices and willingness to participate in the governance process, this research further assumes that the public adopts an active participation strategy with probability $z$ and a passive acceptance strategy with probability $1−z$. When the public adopts the active participation strategy, it manifests as proactively expressing demands through legal channels, such as engaging in community consultations, submitting policy suggestions, and reporting non-compliant flight activities. When the public opts for the passive acceptance strategy, it indicates an attitude of watchfulness, tacit consent, or reluctant acceptance toward low-altitude flight activities, which may stem from information asymmetry, inadequate participation channels, or high costs associated with safeguarding their rights.
- (6)
	In the process of implementing low-altitude airspace governance, the government's regulatory actions entail corresponding resource investments and fiscal expenditures. To more accurately characterize the composition and dynamic changes of government regulatory costs, this research breaks down regulatory costs into fixed costs and variable costs, and further introduces the moderating effect of public participation on the cost structure, thereby constructing a more realistic cost function. Fixed cost $C_{1}$ refers to the rigid expenditure that the government must bear to establish and maintain a basic regulatory system, which does not change with short-term fluctuations in regulatory intensity. Variable cost $\lambda r_{i}$ is directly related to the actual regulatory intensity, reflecting the additional resource consumption incurred by the government under specific regulatory strategies due to increased enforcement frequency, data verification, and technical intervention. Moreover, the public is not merely recipients of regulatory services; their behavioral choices also profoundly affect the government's governance efficiency and cost burden. When the public chooses the active participation strategy with probability $z$, this research introduces a cost reduction ratio $μz$ to quantify the reducing effect of public active participation on government regulatory costs.
- (7)
	Government revenue stems not only from the stable returns derived from routine governance activities but is also jointly influenced by multiple dynamic factors such as the degree of implementation of institutional logics, the level of public participation, and the behavioral choices of enterprises. This research sets parameter $B_{1}$, to represent the government's baseline benefits in the low-altitude economy, reflecting the stable governance outcomes achieved through the orderly utilization of airspace resources, the initial development of the low-altitude industry, and the enhancement of public service capabilities. When the government chooses to implement a strict regulatory strategy and effectively practices state logic, its leading role in maintaining public safety, ensuring airspace operational order, and preventing systemic risks is strengthened. The active exercise of this institutional authority yields additional excess governance performance, denoted as $\alpha_{s} B_{1}$, reflecting the value added by the government in risk prevention and order construction. When the public adopts an active participation strategy, their monitoring behaviors, information feedback mechanisms, and community-based collaborative governance actions significantly enhance the scientific nature of government decision-making and the legitimacy of policies. Consequently, the government gains social collaborative benefits $\alpha_{c} R_{2}$, which are manifested not only as visible benefits such as reduced administrative costs and improved governance efficiency but also encompass intangible values such as enhanced government credibility and improved governance resilience. When enterprises choose a high-input compliance operation strategy, their standardized flight activities effectively reduce the government's regulatory burden and enforcement costs. Meanwhile, enterprises' continuous investment in technology research and development (R&D) accelerates the iterative upgrading of low-altitude aircraft, denoted as the technological progress rate $υ$. This process promotes the overall optimization and industrial maturation of the low-altitude economic ecosystem, subsequently generating positive effects such as industrial scale expansion, tax revenue growth, increased employment opportunities, and enhanced urban innovation competitiveness, which are ultimately translated into macro-economic spillover benefits $\alpha_{m} R_{1}$ enjoyed by the government.
- (8)
	This research sets parameter $C_{2}$ to represent the fundamental operational costs that enterprises must bear when conducting low-altitude flight operations. These costs are rigid expenditures that do not change with adjustments in compliance strategies, covering basic operational outlays such as the procurement expenses for low-altitude aircraft, training investments for flight operators, and daily energy consumption required for operations. They constitute the initial threshold for enterprises to participate in low-altitude economic activities. When enterprises choose a high-investment compliance strategy, they need to incur a series of additional specialized expenditures beyond the fundamental cost $C_{2}$, denoted as the extra compliance cost $\alpha_{m} C_{2}$, in order to meet national regulatory requirements and industry safety standards. These expenditures include safety management system certification, construction of real-time flight data reporting systems, third-party compliance audits, and establishment of emergency response mechanisms, all of which are significantly strategy-dependent. The occurrence of this cost directly reflects the enterprise's proactive adaptation to market rules and regulatory frameworks, serving as a concrete manifestation of market logic in micro-level entity behavior. It also illustrates the enterprise's active management and resource allocation toward compliance risks in the pursuit of long-term sustainable development.
- (9)
	This research defines parameter $B_{2}$ as the baseline operational revenue that enterprises can obtain from conducting low-altitude flight activities under ideal operating conditions, reflecting the potential profitability in optimal scenarios characterized by technological maturity, unobstructed airspace, and regulatory coordination. However, with the rapid growth in the number of low-altitude aircraft and the continuous increase in local airspace usage density, low-altitude airspace congestion will gradually becoming a structural bottleneck that constrains industry development (). To quantify this critical constraint, this research introduces a low-altitude airspace congestion coefficient $ρ$, which characterizes the degree to which airspace usage efficiency declines as density increases. Given the significant inhibitory effect of airspace congestion on operational efficiency, such as flight plan execution rates, mission completion timeliness, and energy consumption, this research dynamically adjusts the baseline revenue $B_{2}$ to $B_{2} \times \left(1 - \rho\right)$ to more accurately reflect the actual achievable revenue of enterprises in real-world congested environments. Furthermore, when enterprises adopt a high-investment compliance strategy, they not only meet basic regulatory requirements but also typically adopt higher technical standards and upgrade management systems, thereby generating additional technology leadership benefits $R_{1} \times \left(1 + \upsilon\right)$. Additionally, active public participation helps enterprises more accurately identify market demands and expand application scenarios. This interaction mechanism can bring additional demand benefits $\alpha_{c} \mathit{dz} B_{2}$ to enterprises, manifested through positive effects such as order growth, enhanced user loyalty, and improved market reputation.
- (10)
	As one of the direct beneficiaries of the low-altitude economy, the public can obtain baseline benefits $B_{3}$, primarily reflected in convenient access to emerging services such as drone logistics, air transportation, and smart inspections, along with positive effects including improved travel efficiency, faster service response, and reduced usage costs. However, if the government adopts a lenient regulatory strategy, it may lead to disordered airspace management, weakened safety standards, or inadequate risk prevention mechanisms, thereby undermining service reliability and public safety perception, resulting in a substantive reduction of public benefits denoted as $\alpha_{s} B_{3}$. When the public chooses an active participation strategy in governance, although requiring investments of time and effort, they can influence policy formulation, and promote transparent operations, thereby enhancing their sense of involvement and control over public affairs and obtaining certain psychological benefits $M$, such as non-economic satisfactions including fulfillment of social responsibility, enhanced trust, and greater sense of environmental control. These benefits constitute an important intrinsic incentive for public participation in governance.
- (11)
	While enjoying the conveniences brought by the low-altitude economy, the public also bears corresponding potential losses $L_{4}$, primarily manifested as various negative externalities. These include safety hazards arising from aircraft operations, risks of personal privacy breaches, and negative impacts on residential environments and psychological well-being such as noise pollution, electromagnetic interference, and visual intrusion (). If enterprises further adopt opportunistic compliance strategies, the frequency and severity of these risks will be significantly exacerbated, leading to additional unexpected losses $\left(1 - \alpha_{m}\right) L_{4}$ for the public, reflected in higher accident exposure probabilities, more prominent privacy infringement issues, and intensified community disturbances. When the public chooses an active participation strategy in governance, although it helps enhance supervisory effectiveness and governance transparency, they must also bear corresponding regulatory participation costs $C_{3}$, covering the time and effort expended on information collection, complaint reporting, community consultations, and organizational coordination. It is worth noting that if enterprises proactively implement high-investment compliance strategy, their standardized operational behaviors will effectively reduce the supervision and protection costs that the public would otherwise incur to mitigate risks. This research sets this mitigation effect as the cost reduction ratio $βy$, meaning that enterprise compliance behaviors can reduce the actual regulatory costs borne by the public from $C_{3}$ to $C_{3} \times \left(1 - \mathit{βy}\right)$, demonstrating the positive spillover effect of enterprise responsibility fulfillment on public burden reduction.
- (12)
	The low-altitude economy is currently still in its early developmental stage, with operational systems and regulatory mechanisms yet to be fully established. Frequent safety incidents have emerged as a prominent risk constraining its sustainable development (). Within this context, this research assumes that when enterprises adopt a high-investment compliance strategy, they achieve relatively high standards in technical specifications and operational management, thereby effectively controlling operational risks. Under such conditions, the probability of safety incidents occurring is $\theta_{1}$. Conversely, when enterprises choose an opportunistic compliance strategy, pursuing short-term benefits by circumventing supervision, concealing flight activities, or reducing safety investments, raising the incident probability to $\theta_{2}$, reflecting the substantial negative impact of low-compliance behavior on safety conditions. Once an incident occurs, the government, enterprises, and the public will inevitably bear corresponding losses: the government faces rising accident handling costs, impaired public governance efficacy, and diminished public trust, recorded as loss $L_{1}$; enterprises must bear direct economic losses, operational disruptions, and damage to brand image, recorded as loss $L_{2}$; while the public endures multiple adverse impacts including threats to personal safety, property damage, psychological trauma, and environmental quality deterioration, recorded as loss $L_{3}$. More importantly, when enterprises choose opportunistic compliance strategies, their behavior not only increases the likelihood of accidents but is also perceived as a challenge to institutional authority and public trust, triggering a punitive linkage mechanism. Under this mechanism, the social consequences of accidents are significantly amplified, with intensified accountability, public opinion pressure, and systemic risks, causing the actual losses of all three parties to double from their original bases. This setting reflects the risk accumulation effects triggered by non-compliant behaviors in low-altitude governance scenarios, highlighting the critical role of corporate compliance choices in maintaining system safety and social stability.

### 3.3. Model analysis

#### 3.3.1. Expected return function

The government, enterprises, and the public can all choose the optimal strategy based on their actual benefits and risk losses. Assuming that the expected returns for the government choosing strict regulation and lenient regulation are $E_{g 1}$ and $E_{g 2}$, respectively, and its average expected return is $\left(\bar{E}\right)_{g}$, then $E_{g 1}$, $E_{g 2}$, $\left(\bar{E}\right)_{g}$ can be expressed as:(1) $\begin{matrix}E_{g 1} & = \mathit{yz} \pi_{g 1} + y \left(1 - z\right) \pi_{g 3} + \left(1 - y\right) z \pi_{g 5} + \left(1 - y\right) \left(1 - z\right) \pi_{g 7} \\ = \left(1 + \alpha_{s}\right) B_{1} + \left(\theta_{2} - \theta_{1}\right) \mathit{yL}_{1} + \left(\mu z^{2} - 1\right) \left(C_{1} + \lambda r_{1}\right) \\ + \mathit{yα}_{m} R_{1} + \mathit{zα}_{c} R_{2} - \theta_{2} L_{1}\end{matrix}$ (2) $\begin{matrix}E_{g 2} & = \mathit{yz} \pi_{g 2} + y \left(1 - z\right) \pi_{g 4} + \left(1 - y\right) z \pi_{g 6} + \left(1 - y\right) \left(1 - z\right) \pi_{g 8} \\ = B_{1} + \left(\mu z^{2} - 1\right) \left(C_{1} + \lambda r_{2}\right) + \mathit{yα}_{m} R_{1} + \mathit{zα}_{c} R_{2} \\ - 2 \mathit{yθ}_{1} L_{1} - 2 \theta_{2} L_{1} + 2 y \theta_{2} L_{1}\end{matrix}$ (3) $\left(\bar{E}\right)_{g} = x E_{g 1} + \left(1 - x\right) E_{g 2}$

Similarly, this research assumes that the expected return for the enterprise choosing a high-investment compliance strategy is $E_{e 1}$, and the expected return for choosing an opportunistic compliance strategy is $E_{e 2}$. Let $\left(\bar{E}\right)_{e}$ represent the average expected return. Then, $E_{e 1}$, $E_{e 2}$, $\left(\bar{E}\right)_{e}$ can be expressed as:(4) $\begin{matrix}E_{e 1} & = \mathit{xz} \pi_{e 1} + \left(1 - x\right) z \pi_{e 2} + \left(1 - z\right) x \pi_{e 3} + \left(1 - x\right) \left(1 - z\right) \pi_{e 4} \\ = \alpha_{c} d z^{2} B_{2} + \left(1 - \rho\right) B_{2} - \left(1 + \alpha_{m}\right) C_{2} + \left(1 + \upsilon\right) R_{1} - \theta_{1} L_{2} + \mathit{xα}_{s} S\end{matrix}$ (5) $\begin{matrix}E_{e 2} & = \mathit{xz} \pi_{e 5} + \left(1 - x\right) z \pi_{e 6} + x \left(1 - z\right) \pi_{e 7} + \left(1 - x\right) \left(1 - z\right) \pi_{e 8} \\ = \alpha_{c} d z^{2} B_{2} + \left(1 - \rho\right) B_{2} - C_{2} - 2 \theta_{2} L_{2} - \mathit{xα}_{s} F\end{matrix}$ (6) $\begin{matrix}\left(\bar{E}\right)_{e} = y E_{e 1} + \left(1 - y\right) E_{e 2}\end{matrix}$

Finally, this research assumes that the expected benefits of the public choosing the active participation strategy and the passive acceptance strategy are $E_{p 1}$ and $E_{p 2}$ respectively, and the average expected benefit of the public is $\left(\bar{E}\right)_{p}$. Then $E_{p 1}$, $E_{p 2}$, and $\left(\bar{E}\right)_{p}$ can be expressed as:(7) $\begin{matrix}E_{p 1} & = \mathit{xy} \pi_{p 1} + \left(1 - x\right) y \pi_{p 2} + x \left(1 - y\right) \pi_{p 5} + \left(1 - x\right) \left(1 - y\right) \pi_{p 6} \\ = \left(1 - \alpha_{s}\right) B_{3} - L_{4} \times \left(1 - \alpha_{m}\right) - C_{3} - L_{4} + \alpha_{c} M - \theta_{2} L_{3} + y L_{4} \\ + \beta y^{2} C_{3} + \alpha_{s} x B_{3} - \alpha_{m} y L_{4} - y \theta_{1} L_{3} + y \theta_{2} L_{3}\end{matrix}$ (8) $\begin{matrix}E_{p 2} & = \mathit{xy} \pi_{p 3} + \left(1 - x\right) y \pi_{p 4} + x \left(1 - y\right) \pi_{p 7} + \left(1 - x\right) \left(1 - y\right) \pi_{p 8} \\ = \left(1 - \alpha_{s}\right) B_{3} - L_{4} - L_{4} \times \left(1 - \alpha_{m}\right) - 2 \theta_{2} L_{3} + y L_{4} + \alpha_{s} x B_{3} \\ - \alpha_{m} y L_{4} - 2 y \theta_{1} L_{3} + 2 y \theta_{2} L_{3}\end{matrix}$ (9) $\begin{matrix}\left(\bar{E}\right)_{p} = z E_{p 1} + \left(1 - z\right) E_{p 2}\end{matrix}$

#### 3.3.2. Stability analysis

The replicator dynamic equation, as a core analytical model of evolutionary game theory, is primarily used to characterize the dynamic evolutionary process of group strategy distribution (). This model quantitatively describes the dynamic changes in group strategy frequency caused by boundedly rational individuals imitating high-payoff strategies through differential equations, thereby enabling the prediction of long-term equilibrium states in group behavior (). Its theoretical essence can be expressed as follows: the growth rate of a specific strategy is positively proportional to its relative fitness, which is the difference between the payoff of that strategy and the group average payoff. The standard form of the replicator dynamic equation can be expressed as:(10) $\begin{matrix}\frac{d x_{i}}{\mathit{dt}} = x_{i} \left(f_{i} \left(x\right) - \bar{f} \left(x\right)\right)\end{matrix}$ where $x_{i}$ denotes the frequency of a strategy, $f_{i} \left(x\right)$ represents the payoff of that strategy, and $\bar{f} \left(x\right)$ stands for the group average payoff.

According to Eq., the replicator dynamic equation for the government $Fx$, enterprises $Fy$, and the public $Fz$ can be expressed as:(11) $\begin{matrix}F \left(x\right) & = \frac{\mathit{dx}}{\mathit{dt}} = x \left(E_{g 1} - \left(\bar{E}\right)_{g}\right) \\ = x \left(1 - x\right) \left(\alpha_{s} B_{1} + \theta_{2} L_{1} + \left(\theta_{1} - \theta_{2}\right) y L_{1} + \lambda \left(\mu z^{2} - 1\right) \left(r_{1} - r_{2}\right)\right)\end{matrix}$ (12) $\begin{matrix}F \left(y\right) & = \frac{\mathit{dy}}{\mathit{dt}} = y \left(E_{e 1} - \left(\bar{E}\right)_{e}\right) \\ = y \left(1 - y\right) \left(\left(1 + \upsilon\right) R_{1} - \alpha_{m} C_{2} - \theta_{1} L_{2} + 2 \theta_{2} L_{2} + \mathit{xα}_{s} \left(S + F\right)\right)\end{matrix}$ (13) $\begin{matrix}F \left(z\right) & = \frac{\mathit{dz}}{\mathit{dt}} = z \left(E_{p 1} - \left(\bar{E}\right)_{p}\right) \\ = z \left(1 - z\right) \left(\alpha_{c} M + \theta_{2} L_{3} + \left(\beta y^{2} - 1\right) C_{3} + \left(\theta_{1} - \theta_{2}\right) y L_{3}\right)\end{matrix}$

By setting the replication dynamic equations $Fx=0$, $Fy=0$, and $Fz=0$ to hold simultaneously and solving the system for equilibrium solutions, this research identifies a total of 14 system stable points. These stable points represent the long-term states toward which the game system may converge or settle. Among them, there are eight pure-strategy equilibrium points: $E_{1} = \left(0,0,0\right)$, $E_{2} = \left(1,0,0\right)$, $E_{3} = \left(0,1,0\right)$, $E_{4} = \left(0,0,1\right)$, $E_{5} = \left(1,1,0\right)$, $E_{6} = \left(1,0,1\right)$, $E_{7} = \left(0,1,1\right)$, and $E_{8} = \left(1,1,1\right)$. Pure-strategy equilibrium points correspond to fully deterministic combinations of the three parties' strategy choices, where each party adopts an all-or-nothing extreme strategy, forming the eight vertices of the strategy space and carrying clear real-world policy implications. Additionally, the system exhibits six mixed-strategy equilibrium points: $E_{9} = \left(x_{9}^{*},y_{9}^{*},0\right)$, $E_{10} = \left(x_{10}^{*},y_{10}^{*},1\right)$, $E_{11} = \left(x_{11}^{*},y_{11}^{*},z_{11}^{*}\right)$, $E_{12} = \left(x_{12}^{*},y_{12}^{*},z_{12}^{*}\right)$, $E_{13} = \left(x_{13}^{*},y_{13}^{*},z_{13}^{*}\right)$, and $E_{14} = \left(x_{14}^{*},y_{14}^{*},z_{14}^{*}\right)$. Mixed-strategy equilibrium points represent intermediate states in which the government, enterprises, and the public randomly select their strategies according to specific probability distributions. Such equilibria typically arise in contexts characterized by multi-force gaming, frequent strategy adjustments, or incomplete information, reflecting transitional or critical states during the system's evolutionary process.

According to the stability criterion of evolutionary game theory, a necessary and sufficient condition for an equilibrium point to qualify as an evolutionarily stable strategy is that it must constitute a strict Nash equilibrium (). A strict Nash equilibrium implies that under such a strategy profile, any unilateral deviation by any party would lead to a decrease in its own fitness, thereby endowing the system with inherent resistance to disturbances (). Existing research indicates that strict Nash equilibria can only occur in pure-strategy combinations, whereas mixed-strategy equilibria, due to the existence of room for strategic adjustments, generally lack evolutionary stability and often manifest as saddle points or unstable equilibria, serving merely as transitional features along the system's evolutionary path (; ). Therefore, in asymmetric multi-agent evolutionary game analysis, although multiple stable solutions may exist mathematically, those with practical significance and capable of representing long-term stable outcomes are primarily the pure-strategy equilibrium points that satisfy the conditions for local asymptotic stability. While mixed-strategy equilibria contribute to understanding the dynamic complexity of the system, they are unlikely to become the final form of an actual governance structure. Given that this research focuses on the stability of pure strategy equilibrium points, during the construction of the replicator dynamics equations and the derivation of the Jacobian matrix, terms corresponding to certain parameters (such as $ρ$) are naturally eliminated in the partial derivative operations. This mathematical characteristic does not diminish the importance of such parameters, instead, it reveals their unique functional mechanism: these parameters primarily regulate the participation thresholds of game agents rather than directly influencing the system's resilience to perturbations near local equilibria (; ).

Based on the above theoretical foundation, this research will subsequently focus on analyzing the asymptotic stability of the aforementioned eight pure-strategy equilibrium points. Based on the systematic analysis of the four typical equilibrium scenarios in above, this research further refines and constructs four core propositions with theoretical depth and practical implications grounded in institutional logics theory. These propositions not only reveal the intrinsic mechanisms and interactive dynamics among the three institutional logics of state, market, and community in low-altitude airspace governance, but also delve into the dominance, limitations, and synergistic conditions of each logic across different contexts.

**Proposition 1**

The government's decision to enforce strict regulation is primarily moderated by the intensity of state logic ($\alpha_{s}$). When $\alpha_{s} > max \left(\frac{\lambda \left(r_{1} - r_{2}\right) - \theta _{2} L _{1} \left]\right.}{B _{1}},\frac{\lambda \left(r _{1} - r _{2}\right) - \mathtt{\theta} _{1} L _{1}}{B _{1}}\right)$, regardless of the strategic combinations adopted by enterprises and the public, the government will resolutely choose and maintain strict regulation, highlighting the institutional rigidity and priority of state logic in the governance system.

**Explanation:** A comparative analysis of the four equilibrium scenarios consistently demonstrates that whether the government adopts a strict regulatory strategy does not entirely depend on enterprises' compliance willingness or public participation levels. Instead, the key lies in whether the state logic intensity parameter $\alpha_{s}$ surpasses a certain critical threshold. This phenomenon profoundly reflects the institutional rigidity characteristic of state logic, that is, in areas involving national security, significant public interests, and systemic risks, state actions exhibit non-negotiability in objectives and priority in execution. Even if enterprises lean toward opportunism driven by market logic, or the public remains silent due to high monitoring costs, the government can unilaterally initiate and sustain high-intensity governance interventions by leveraging its monopolistic authoritative resources and institutional legitimacy.

**Proposition 2**

Enterprises' compliance decisions are significantly moderated by the intensity of market logic ($\alpha_{m}$) and exhibit a profound sensitivity paradox: while high $\alpha_{m}$ enhances their responsiveness to policy incentives and market returns, it simultaneously amplifies their tendency to avoid compliance costs. The critical condition for enterprises to choose high-investment compliance is $\alpha_{m} < \frac{\left(1 + \upsilon\right) R_{1} + \alpha_{s} \left(F + S\right) + \left(2 \theta_{2} - \theta_{1}\right) L_{2}}{C_{2}}$.

**Explanation:** A comparative analysis of the four equilibrium scenarios reveals that the fundamental reason enterprises choose high-investment compliance in Scenarios 2 and 4, while adopting opportunistic strategies in Scenarios 1 and 3, lies in whether the intensity of market logic $\alpha_{m}$ falls below the aforementioned critical threshold. This structure uncovers a seemingly contradictory yet highly pragmatic phenomenon: when enterprises are deeply embedded in market logic, they exhibit heightened sensitivity to policy signals and technological advancements, which theoretically favors policy guidance. However, this strong market orientation also makes them highly vigilant against any expenditures not directly generating revenue, particularly compliance investments with returns that are difficult to quantify in the short term. Consequently, the findings of this research indicate that the higher $\alpha_{m}$ is, the more likely enterprises are to perceive compliance as a cost burden rather than a strategic investment, thereby intensifying their motivation to avoid it.

**Proposition 3**

Public participation decisions are jointly moderated by the intensity of community logic ($\alpha_{c}$) and the perception of safety risks ($\theta_{i} L_{3}$). The fundamental reason for this behavioral divergence lies in whether the aforementioned dual threshold conditions are met. Therefore, enhancing the effectiveness of public participation requires not only strengthening citizens' sense of belonging to boost $\alpha_{c}$ but also improving enterprise transparency ($β$) through measures such as mandatory information disclosure and robust whistleblower protection mechanisms, thereby substantively reducing the costs and risks of public oversight.

**Proposition 4**

The realization of the ideal tripartite synergistic state relies on the precise coupling and dynamic balance among the three institutional logics of state, market, and community, requiring the simultaneous satisfaction of the following three structural conditions: $\alpha_{s} > \frac{\lambda \left(1 - \mu\right) \left(r_{1} - r_{2}\right) - \theta_{1} L_{1}}{B_{1}}$, $\alpha_{m} < \frac{\left(1 + \upsilon\right) R_{1} + \alpha_{s} \left(F + S\right) + \left(2 \theta_{2} - \theta_{1}\right) L_{2}}{C_{2}}$, and $\alpha_{c} > \frac{\left(1 - \beta\right) C_{3} - \theta_{1} L_{3}}{M}$.

**Explanation:** As the only Pareto-optimal equilibrium in the evolutionary game system that achieves tripartite synergy, Scenario 4 represents an ideal vision for modern complex governance. However, this ideal state is not naturally emergent but highly dependent on the precise alignment and functional complementarity of the three institutional logics within specific parameter ranges. Its existence reveals a core governance principle: effective collaborative governance is neither the dominant control of a single entity nor the simple aggregation of independent actions by various actors. ### 4.2. Global sensitivity analysis

To comprehensively evaluate the robustness of the evolutionary game model and deeply reveal the influence paths and effect intensities of various input parameters on the system's equilibrium outcomes, this research employs the Sobol method for global sensitivity analysis. This method is a variance decomposition-based global analysis technique widely applied in complex system modeling (), biology science (), and environmental science (). It is particularly suitable for model systems characterized by nonlinearity, non-monotonicity, and high-dimensional parameter interactions. Based on the Sobol quasi-Monte Carlo sequence, this research generated 2048 sample points $\left(N = 2^{11} = 2048\right)$. The sample size was chosen as a power of two based on the mathematical properties of the Sobol sequence: under this condition, the sequence exhibits optimal space-filling capacity and faster convergence, significantly improving the accuracy and computational efficiency of variance decomposition (; ). Each sample includes a random combination of all parameters to be analyzed in the model. These combinations are substituted into the tripartite evolutionary game dynamics equations to simulate the strategic evolution paths of each participating entity. The final convergence of the system to the ideal synergistic state (Scenario 4) is recorded and analyzed as the core output variable.

The core principle of the Sobol method lies in systematically decomposing the total variance of the model output variable, thereby quantifying the relative contribution of each input parameter and their higher-order interactions to the output uncertainty (). Specifically, assuming the model output $Y=fX$ is a deterministic function of the input parameter vector $\mathbf{\mathit{X}} = \left(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}\right)$, and each parameter varies independently within its domain. The Sobol method, based on the Hoeffding variance decomposition theorem, decomposes the total variance $D=VarY$ into several orthogonal components (). These components correspond to the main effects of individual parameters, the second-order interaction effects between pairs of parameters, and the sum of higher-order synergistic or antagonistic effects of multiple parameters, thereby enabling a clear isolation and quantitative assessment of various sources of influence ().(23) $\begin{matrix}f \left(\mathbf{\mathit{X}}\right) & = f_{0} + \underset{i}{\sum} f_{i} \left(X_{i}\right) + \underset{i < j}{\sum} f_{\mathit{ij}} \left(X_{i},X_{j}\right) \\ + \underset{i < j < k}{\sum} f_{\mathit{ijk}} \left(X_{i},X_{j},X_{k}\right) + \hdots + f_{12 \ldots n} \left(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\right)\end{matrix}$ (24) $\begin{matrix}D = \mathit{Var} \left(Y\right) = \underset{i}{\sum} D_{i} + \underset{i < j}{\sum} D_{\mathit{ij}} + \underset{i < j < k}{\sum} D_{\mathit{ijk}} + \hdots + D_{12 \ldots n}\end{matrix}$

On this basis, two types of key sensitivity indices are defined: the first-order sensitivity index $S_{i}$, which measures the main effect of parameter $X_{i}$ on the output variance, reflecting the proportion of output variation explained by changing this parameter independently without considering interactions with other parameters; and the total-effect index $S T_{i}$, which expands this measurement by incorporating not only the main effect of $X_{i}$ but also the combined contributions of all possible higher-order interactions with other parameters, thereby providing a more comprehensive representation of the parameter's overall impact on model output uncertainty. When the total-effect index $S T_{i}$ of a parameter is significantly higher than its first-order index $S_{i}$, it indicates that the parameter primarily influences system behavior through synergistic, moderating, or nonlinear interactive mechanisms with other factors, suggesting its potential critical role as an indirect or structural element in complex evolutionary processes ().(25) $\begin{matrix}S_{i} = \frac{D_{i}}{D}\end{matrix}$ (26) $\begin{matrix}S T_{i} = \frac{D_{i} + \underset{j \neq i}{\sum} D_{\mathit{ij}} + \underset{j < k , j , k \neq i}{\sum} D_{\mathit{ijk}} + \hdots}{D}\end{matrix}$

Based on the aforementioned theoretical framework and methodological design, this research conducted a systematic Sobol global sensitivity analysis of the low-altitude airspace collaborative governance model under the tripartite evolutionary game framework using the Python platform. The results are shown in and. The analysis reveals that the evolutionary game model of low-altitude airspace governance constructed in this research is a complex adaptive system characterized by high nonlinearity and significant coupling effects among parameters. For the majority of parameters, especially the most influential ones (such as $C_{2}$, $λ$, and $\alpha_{s}$), their first-order sensitivity indices $S_{i}$ are significantly lower than their corresponding total-effect indices $S T_{i}$. This phenomenon profoundly indicates that, within this governance system, the independent variation of a single parameter has a relatively limited impact on the overall system output. However, once these parameters interact with other factors, they can generate significant synergistic amplification or suppression effects through nonlinear mechanisms, thereby dominantly shaping the evolutionary trajectory of the system. This strong interactivity and nonlinearity fundamentally stem from the multiple nonlinear terms and parameter product structures introduced during the model construction. This also implies that policy design or mechanism optimization based solely on a single-variable perspective may fail to accurately capture the actual operational logic of the system. Only by fully considering the interactions among parameters and the coupling structure of the system as a whole can key regulatory leverage points be effectively identified, enhancing the precision and robustness of governance strategies.

## 5\. Discussion

### 5.1. Research conclusions

Grounded in institutional logics theory, this research develops a tripartite evolutionary game model for collaborative governance of low-altitude airspace, tailored to the emerging context of the low-altitude economy. The research aims to elucidate the intrinsic mechanisms and dynamic evolutionary pathways of low-altitude airspace collaborative governance within complex institutional environments and multi-stakeholder interactions. The model aligns the three core actors in the governance system—government, enterprises, and the public—with three dominant institutional logics: state logic, market logic, and community logic. To enhance the model's real-world explanatory power and policy relevance, this research selects Shenzhen, China, as an empirical case for contextual embedding. Building on this foundation, systematic global and local sensitivity analyses are conducted to identify key variables driving the system's evolutionary trajectory and their complex interactive mechanisms. Through a series of theoretical deductions and numerical simulation experiments, the present study derives the following key conclusions:
- (1)
	The model stability analysis reveals four typical equilibrium scenarios and their corresponding institutional logic propositions. By conducting eigenvalue analysis on the Jacobian matrix of the replication dynamic equations and performing categorical discussions based on parameter constraints, this research identifies four stable equilibrium states with practical explanatory power. In these four scenarios, the government consistently tends to adopt a strict regulatory strategy, highlighting the dominant role of state logic in low-altitude airspace governance. In contrast, the strategic choices of enterprises and the public are highly dependent on specific parameter combinations. Building on this, the study further integrates institutional logic theory to propose four key propositions, clearly defining the threshold conditions that the state logic coefficient $\alpha_{s}$, the market logic coefficient $\alpha_{m}$, and the community logic coefficient $\alpha_{c}$ must satisfy to drive the system toward an ideal synergistic equilibrium. This provides a clear theoretical framework for understanding how institutional environments structurally shape the behavior of multiple actors.
- (2)
	The global sensitivity analysis reveals a multi-level and multi-dimensional driving structure of the system. The results indicate that the enterprise's basic operational cost $C_{2}$ and the marginal cost coefficient of regulatory intensity $λ$ are the two parameters with the highest total-effect indices, constituting the most sensitive regulatory levers in the system. Following closely, the community logic coefficient $\alpha_{c}$ and the state logic coefficient $\alpha_{s}$ exhibit typical interaction-dominant characteristics: their first-order sensitivity indices are extremely low, while their total-effect indices are significantly prominent. This indicates that they do not influence the system through independent effects but act as institutional amplifiers, indirectly yet profoundly shaping the strategic evolution paths of the three parties by modulating the perceived effectiveness of other benefit or cost parameters. Additionally, the government's basic benefit $B_{1}$ and the efficiency coefficient of public participation in reducing regulatory costs $μ$ demonstrate strong performance in both first-order and total effects. They not only serve direct incentive functions but also act as key hubs connecting the interactions among the government, enterprises, and the public, highlighting the dual foundational roles of economic rationality and social synergy in governance efficacy.
- (3)
	Local sensitivity analysis precisely delineates the critical mechanisms through which key parameters trigger systemic transitions. The research finds that changes in the enterprise's basic operational cost $C_{2}$ and the public's additional psychological benefit $M$ can directly alter the system's final equilibrium state. Specifically, when $C_{2}$ exceeds a critical threshold, enterprises shift from high-investment compliance to opportunistic behavior due to excessive cost pressure. Conversely, when $M$ surpasses its threshold, their willingness to participate is effectively activated, enabling the system to transition to the ideal tripartite synergistic equilibrium point $E_{8} \left(1,1,1\right)$. This result not only validates the fundamental role of cost-benefit mechanisms in driving actor strategies but also precisely identifies the critical turning point from government-enterprise collaboration to tripartite co-governance, providing a quantifiable basis for policy design and intervention.
- (4)
	The role of institutional logic coefficients is primarily reflected in the evolutionary process rather than the final equilibrium. Although the state logic coefficient $\alpha_{s}$, market logic coefficient $\alpha_{m}$, and community logic coefficient $\alpha_{c}$ are core variables in this research, their joint variation under the constraint $\alpha_{s} + \alpha_{m} + \alpha_{c} = 1$ does not alter the system's final stable state. However, their internal allocation significantly influences the shape of the evolutionary trajectory and the convergence speed. Specifically, a higher $\alpha_{s}$ leads to faster system convergence, reflecting the anchoring role of state authority in stabilizing order at the current governance stage. While increases in $\alpha_{m}$ and $\alpha_{c}$ alone are insufficient to trigger equilibrium transitions, they enhance social mobilization potential and market responsiveness, thereby building institutional resilience and social momentum for future transitions to higher-order collaborative governance states.

### 5.2. Practical implications

This research takes Shenzhen's pioneering practices in low-altitude economy governance as a key empirical foundation, uncovering the core mechanisms of the co-evolution among government regulation, enterprise behavior, and public participation. Although Shenzhen, as a forefront of low-altitude economy development, possesses unique advantages such as vibrant technological innovation, an inclusive policy environment, and a well-established market mechanism, the theoretical framework and core conclusions of this research still hold universal significance beyond regional boundaries. In light of the differences among cities at various tiers in terms of resource endowments, development stages, and governance capacities, the following points provide cross-contextual reference pathways for optimizing low-altitude economy governance in different regions:
- (1)
	Establish a tiered and categorized precise subsidy mechanism for enterprises, and set subsidy thresholds according to local conditions. This research reveals that enterprises' basic operating cost $C_{2}$ is a sensitive variable affecting the evolution of the system. Although the Shenzhen case identified a specific critical value of $C_{2}$, other cities should, when formulating subsidy policies, establish differentiated monitoring benchmarks for $C_{2}$ and subsidy trigger conditions based on their own development stages, industrial foundations, enterprise size structures, and fiscal affordability. By developing localized cost-benefit simulation models, cities can precisely target subsidy intensities and support nodes, ensuring that policies are both economically sustainable and effective in incentivizing enterprises to fulfill compliance and innovation functions. This approach enables a balance between governance efficiency and industrial vitality across different institutional environments.
- (2)
	Advance the development of a digital regulatory system tailored to local conditions to reduce the marginal cost of regulation $λ$. Digital regulation is a key means of enhancing efficiency and lowering institutional costs (). For cities with a strong technological foundation and greater fiscal capacity, they can take the lead in deploying low-altitude UAV traffic management systems, deeply integrating cutting-edge technologies such as artificial intelligence and blockchain to achieve real-time, trusted flow of airspace data. For cities with relatively weaker foundations, a modular and gradual digital path can be adopted, prioritizing the digitalization of key processes such as airspace use applications and basic information collection, thereby progressively improving regulatory precision and response efficiency. Regardless of a city's development stage, enhancing institutional implementation efficiency by reducing $λ$ helps create a transparent and predictable policy environment, strengthens enterprises' willingness to comply, and reinforces system stability.
- (3)
	Encourage innovations in models of public participation across different regions, fostering value identification mechanisms tailored to local socio-psychological characteristics. This research identifies the additional psychological benefit for the public $M$, as a key variable in achieving deep-level collaborative governance. Practices in Shenzhen indicate that activities such as low-altitude flight performances and safety education can significantly enhance the public's sense of belonging and gain. When designing public participation strategies, other cities can flexibly develop engagement formats by integrating local cultural traditions, community structures, and public awareness levels. Examples include creating low-altitude cultural festivals with local characteristics, supporting community self-governance organizations in airspace supervision, and developing mobile feedback platforms tailored to local usage habits. These approaches effectively strengthen the public's perception of their role as governance subjects, thereby surpassing the critical threshold of $M$ and activating the institutional amplification effect of community logic $\alpha_{c}$.
- (4)
	Optimize the proportional relationship among the triple logics of the state, the market, and the community to shape a robust and resilient governance path. Shenzhen's experience shows that appropriately strengthening the state logic $\alpha_{s}$ helps accelerate system convergence and stabilize expectations. In different cities, the weighting of these three logics needs to be carefully balanced based on factors such as their development stage, industrial structure, and cultural traditions. In regions with relatively underdeveloped institutional foundations, the order-anchoring role of state logic can be leveraged by appropriately strengthening regulatory supply and cross-departmental coordination. In areas with high market vitality and strong innovation capacity, the operational space for market logic $\alpha_{m}$ can be expanded, while ensuring safety, by exploring pilot mechanisms like regulatory sandboxes. In regions rich in social capital and with a strong sense of civic awareness, the participation of community logic $\alpha_{c}$ can be enhanced to promote the formation of a diversified governance pattern featuring government guidance, market enablement, and social co-governance.
- (5)
	Promote the establishment of dynamic monitoring and policy adjustment mechanisms that are both universally applicable and flexible. It is recommended that cities, building upon their own governance foundations, construct digital monitoring platforms for the low-altitude economy tailored to local needs. These platforms should dynamically track and analyze trends in key parameters, such as enterprise operational cost $C_{2}$, public psychological benefit $M$, and marginal regulatory cost $λ$. The platforms should be equipped with functions for multi-scenario simulation, sensitivity assessment, and policy early warning, supporting decision-makers in initiating timely policy responses when parameters approach critical thresholds. Concurrently, regular policy review and iteration mechanisms should be established to continuously optimize the parameter systems and supporting measures based on local practical feedback. This will continuously enhance the scientific rigor, adaptability, and foresight of the governance system, providing institutional guarantees for the healthy and orderly development of the low-altitude economy nationwide.

In summary, although this research conducts an in-depth analysis using Shenzhen as a typical sample, the system evolution logic, key sensitive variables, and policy action mechanisms it reveals possess cross-regional reference value. In the future, when drawing on Shenzhen's experience, other regions should fully consider their local development conditions and governance capabilities to explore low-altitude economic governance models with local characteristics, collectively promoting the formation of a new pattern of low-altitude governance characterized by diversified symbiosis and co-evolution.

### 5.3. Research limitations and future directions

This research provides a robust theoretical framework and insights for understanding collaborative governance in low-altitude airspace through the construction of a tripartite evolutionary game model. However, it still faces the following limitations:

Firstly, there is the challenge of simplifying real-world complexity. Although the tripartite evolutionary game model developed in the present study successfully abstracts the behaviors of the government, enterprises, and the public into a ternary framework of state logic, market logic, and community logic based on institutional logic theory, this framework inevitably simplifies the more intricate interactions among stakeholders in reality. For instance, the participation of diverse actors such as military departments, civil aviation authorities, and local grassroots organizations may be oversimplified into a monolithic government role. This simplification leads to an inadequate representation of practical issues like civil-military coordination and the division of responsibilities between central and local governments, thereby limiting the model's depth in understanding multi-level, cross-sectoral collaboration mechanisms in the real world.

Secondly, the difficulty in quantifying highly abstract concepts. The introduction of institutional logic coefficients represents a major innovation in this research. However, precisely quantifying these highly abstract concepts poses a significant challenge. The current research primarily relies on the expert consultation method to determine these coefficients. While this approach can reflect real-world conditions to a certain extent, further methodological breakthroughs are still required to robustly correlate and calibrate these coefficients with observable empirical data at the practical level. Future work needs to explore more effective measurement tools and techniques to enhance the scientific rigor and accuracy of model parameter settings.

Thirdly, the gap between static assumptions and dynamic realities. Although the sensitivity analysis helped identify the regulatory effects of key parameters, the model's parameter settings are still based on static assumptions. In reality, technological iterations in the low-altitude economy, dynamic policy adjustments, and unforeseen events may all lead to nonlinear disturbances in the parameters. For example, the model assumes that public participation can directly reduce government regulatory costs, which aligns with the logic of a mature co-governance structure. However, in the early stages of low-altitude economy development, public participation might instead increase the government's administrative burden due to processes such as investigation, evidence collection, and feedback handling, creating a nonlinear effect that first increases and then decreases. The current model fails to establish a dynamic parameter feedback mechanism, making it difficult to simulate such nonlinear disturbances and the interaction effects between policy interventions and changes in the external environment. This implies that the model may have a certain lag when responding to rapidly changing real-world situations.

In response to the aforementioned limitations, future research could be deepened and expanded in the following directions:
- (1)
	Expansion to a multi-agent evolutionary game framework. Future models could consider constructing a quaternary or even more extensive multi-agent evolutionary game framework by incorporating key actors such as military departments, civil aviation authorities, and industry associations. This expansion would also allow for the exploration of competition and integration among different logics within individual actors. Such an approach would more accurately reflect the complex landscape of low-altitude governance, particularly in addressing issues related to cross-departmental coordination and multi-level governance.
- (2)
	Strengthening the integration of theoretical and empirical research. A key focus for future work is to more closely integrate theoretical models with empirical studies. Through methods such as large-scale questionnaire surveys, expert interviews, policy text analysis, and social media big data analysis, indirect measurement and assignment can be attempted for key variables such as institutional logic coefficients and psychological benefits. This will not only enhance the model's practical explanatory power but also provide customized policy simulation tools for different regions, supporting the development of more targeted local governance strategies.
- (3)
	Introducing dynamic adaptive mechanisms. To better reflect the dynamic characteristics of the real world, future research could consider incorporating agents' learning behaviors, the dynamic impact of technological progress, and the processes of policy experimentation and adjustment into the model framework. In particular, the mechanism of public participation exhibits significant stage-based characteristics, and such nonlinear transition processes are difficult to capture with static parameters. Therefore, subsequent research could employ repeated game models with adaptive parameters or combine them with agent-based simulations to model how the system evolves from a high-cost participation stage to an efficient co-governance stage under dynamically changing environments. Such improvements would not only enhance the predictive power of the model but also provide a reference basis for formulating forward-looking and adaptable long-term strategies.
