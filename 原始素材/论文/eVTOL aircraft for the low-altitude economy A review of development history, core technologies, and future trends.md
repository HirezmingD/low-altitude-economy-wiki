## 1\. Introduction

With the continuous advancement of science and ongoing technological breakthroughs, the concept of the low-altitude economy has emerged and is gradually becoming a key strategic emerging industry. It plays a crucial role in promoting the transformation and upgrading of the aerospace industry and in building a modern, integrated three-dimensional transportation system (). The term “low-altitude” refers to airspace that generally lies below 1; 000 m in vertical range, with specific height boundaries defined according to regional characteristics and practical needs (). The low-altitude economy is a comprehensive economic model driven by various manned and unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) operations within low-altitude airspace. It stimulates the integrated development of related sectors (, ), encompassing a wide range of fields such as battery and flight systems, agricultural inspection, logistics and express delivery, tourism, and aviation. The industry itself comprises four major categories: manufacturing, flight operations, support services, and integrated services, as illustrated in.

The low-altitude economy is primarily driven by electric Vertical Take-Off and Landing (eVTOL) aircraft and UAVs. eVTOLs, characterized by their green, low-carbon footprint, vertical take-off and landing (VTOL) capabilities, and high operational flexibility, are capable of operating in various complex and non-permissive environments. They enable precise point-to-point flight missions, thereby significantly enhancing the connectivity of low-altitude airspace while effectively mitigating traffic congestion issues on the ground (). According to the Vertical Flight Society of the United States, eVTOLs represent a new mode of urban air transportation that is “cleaner, quieter, safer, and more versatile” (), with primary applications in three major domains: passenger transport, cargo delivery, and urban management. illustrates the market forecast for eVTOL applications in both passenger and cargo transport. In the near term, the eVTOL market is expected to be primarily driven by passenger operations. By 2040, both passenger and cargo segments are projected to undergo exponential growth, ultimately reaching a relatively balanced state. In the passenger transport sector, current developments largely resemble traditional helicopter operations, as eVTOL products are still undergoing certification and airworthiness approval processes (). In the cargo delivery domain, particularly express logistics, eVTOLs are among the most widely adopted commercial services. They offer an effective solution to relieve pressure on ground traffic and improve the efficiency of goods distribution (). In the domain of urban management, both helicopters and UAVs have already seen extensive deployment and are capable of efficiently performing a variety of operational tasks (). As the eVTOL technology system continues to mature, its role within the low-altitude economy is becoming increasingly prominent. It not only accelerates the commercialization of urban air mobility (UAM), but also drives the concurrent development of low-altitude airspace management systems, supporting infrastructure, operational standards, and regulatory frameworks. Consequently, eVTOLs are expected to become a vital component in enabling the high-quality development of the low-altitude economy and in constructing an integrated air-ground three-dimensional transportation network ().

The low-altitude economy is experiencing rapid development on a global scale (). illustrates the projected market size of UAM, with forecasts indicating that the low-altitude economy is expected to exhibit exponential growth, highlighting its substantial development potential in the emerging aviation sector. The United States holds a leading position in this domain, with the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) having established a mature regulatory framework for low-altitude airspace management that supports the development of UAM and eVTOL aircraft (). Companies such as Wing and Joby Aviation are at the forefront of low-altitude delivery and eVTOL technology globally. These firms have received FAA flight test approvals and aim to achieve commercial operations by 2030 (). Europe is also actively advancing its low-altitude economy. The European Union has launched the U-space initiative, which seeks to develop regulatory and operational standards for low-altitude airspace management (). In countries like Germany and the United Kingdom, several companies are piloting urban air mobility projects across multiple cities, thereby accelerating the development and deployment of eVTOLs. In Asia, both Japan and South Korea have made strategic investments in this field. Japan plans to showcase urban air mobility using eVTOLs at the 2025 Osaka Expo, while South Korea has developed a comprehensive urban air mobility roadmap and conducted multiple rounds of flight testing. China’s low-altitude economy is also expanding rapidly. In 2024, the term “low-altitude economy” was included for the first time in the national government work report (), signaling its elevation to a national strategic emerging industry. Cities such as Shenzhen, Jiangsu, Shanghai, and Beijing have introduced a range of supportive policies, forming mature industrial chains and establishing themselves as pilot regions for the development of the low-altitude economy and the eVTOL industry ().

## 2\. Development history and current status

The development of the low-altitude economy has significantly accelerated the growth of related enterprises (), particularly evident in the technological breakthroughs and commercialization progress of midstream eVTOL aircraft manufacturers. In 2009, the U.S. startup Joby Aviation was founded, emerging as an early pioneer in the eVTOL sector. The company took the lead in establishing a presence within the low-altitude economy and has since made continuous progress in key technologies such as aircraft design and electric propulsion systems (). In 2011, the Italian helicopter manufacturer AgustaWestland formally introduced the eVTOL concept, marking a new stage of maturity for the low-altitude economy industry (). In 2016, ride-sharing giant Uber launched its “Uber Elevate” urban air mobility initiative, proposing the use of eVTOLs for aerial taxi services in urban environments—an initiative that catalyzed the transition of eVTOLs from conceptual validation toward commercial implementation (). In the same year, the U.S. FAA introduced a performance-based revision to Part 23 of its airworthiness standards, laying an institutional foundation for eVTOL certification (). That year, Joby also successfully completed its Series A funding round, securing $130 million. In 2019, the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) issued a regulatory framework for eVTOL operations and airworthiness, establishing a certification system tailored to the technical characteristics of these aircraft (). In the same year, EHang, a Guangzhou-based intelligent aviation technology company, was successfully listed on the NASDAQ, becoming the world's first publicly traded eVTOL enterprise—a milestone that underscored China’s emerging influence in this rapidly evolving sector (). The year 2021 marked a peak in capital investment in the eVTOL industry, with several UAM companies going public via Special Purpose Acquisition Companies (SPACs), including Vertical Aerospace (June 10, 2021), Joby Aviation (August 11, 2021), Lilium (September 15, 2021), and Archer Aviation (September 17, 2021; ). In 2023, the commercial application of eVTOLs made substantial progress. Opener completed its first flight with a commercial customer, demonstrating the maturity and operational potential of its technology. EHang received the world’s first eVTOL Type Certificate (TC), representing a major breakthrough for China's eVTOL industry within the international aviation certification framework (). In November of the same year, Joby's S4 eVTOL aircraft successfully conducted a demonstration flight in the core area of Manhattan, New York City (, ), marking a new phase of operational validation in dense urban environments and further affirming the feasibility and safety of eVTOLs in future urban air mobility systems. By 2024, multiple countries accelerated reforms of low-altitude airspace and infrastructure development. Pilot programs for urban low-altitude air routes were initiated in China, the United States, Japan, South Korea, and other regions (). The overall development trajectory is illustrated in.

## 3\. Key technologies of eVTOL

The concept of VTOL aircraft can be traced back to the early 20th century, with the first prototype validation achieved in the 1940 s (). However, the widespread practical application of such aircraft continues to face numerous challenges. These include the complexity of aerodynamic design, the technical difficulty of electric propulsion systems, limitations in flight endurance, underdeveloped air traffic regulatory frameworks, and public acceptance issues (). Consequently, only a limited number of VTOL aircraft types have been implemented in urban transportation scenarios to date (). With ongoing advancements in electric propulsion, long-endurance battery technology, and intelligent flight systems, eVTOL has emerged as a prominent trend and a key modality for future urban air mobility. Compared to traditional helicopters, eVTOL offers significant advantages in noise reduction, operational and maintenance costs, safety redundancy design, and environmental sustainability, as shown in. Given its broad development prospects, a systematic study of the technological pathways of eVTOL is critical to accelerating its engineering application and industrial implementation. This chapter provides an in-depth analysis of the key technologies of eVTOL aircraft from seven perspectives: flight performance metrics, overall aerodynamic configuration design, distributed electric propulsion architecture, long-endurance battery systems, intelligent autonomous flight, digital low-altitude traffic management systems, and airworthiness and safety. Collectively, these seven technological pillars constitute the core framework that ensures the efficient, safe, and intelligent operation of eVTOL systems.

The comparison demonstrates that Joby S2 exhibits significant advantages across multiple performance indicators relative to both the R22 and the RV-7. The Joby S2 employs a fully electric propulsion system, which substantially reduces energy consumption and operational costs per unit of flight time. Although its purchase price is slightly higher than that of the RV-7, the lower failure rate and reduced maintenance costs associated with the electric drivetrain confer a more competitive total lifecycle cost. With a maximum takeoff weight of approximately 2,000 kg—higher than the R22 (1,370 kg) and RV-7 (1,800 kg)—the Joby S2 compensates through an efficient power distribution system and advanced aerodynamic design, which help mitigate energy consumption associated with increased payload and, to a certain extent, enhance mission payload capacity. While its maximum range of approximately 200 km falls short of that of the RV-7, it remains sufficient for urban and suburban commuting needs. The overall aerodynamic configuration design of eVTOL aircraft significantly influences their flight performance, energy efficiency, and mission adaptability. Compared to conventional fixed-wing or rotorcraft configurations, eVTOL platforms must efficiently transition between vertical takeoff and landing (VTOL) and horizontal cruise flight modes, which imposes more stringent requirements on aerodynamic design (). Currently, the aerodynamic configurations of eVTOL aircraft are primarily categorized into multirotor, compound wing, tiltrotor, and tilt-ducted fan configurations (). #### 3.2.1. Multirotor eVTOL

Multirotor eVTOL aircraft are characterized by relatively simple structural configurations and mature control strategies (). Typically, this configuration employs electric propulsion systems, with multiple electric motors distributed across the airframe to independently drive propellers. This design enables highly distributed and redundant flight control. Several representative multirotor eVTOL aircraft are illustrated in.

![](https://ars.els-cdn.com/content/image/1-s2.0-S2213624X25002664-gr5.jpg)

Download: Download high-res image (149KB)

Multirotor eVTOLs prioritize hover efficiency and low-speed flight performance. During vertical takeoff and landing, the rotors generate all necessary lift, and they continue to provide all thrust and lift during cruise flight. Consequently, their rotor design typically targets high lift coefficients and thrust-to-weight ratios to ensure stable vertical takeoff, landing, and hover capabilities (). In addition, the rapid response and high precision of electric propulsion systems enhance maneuverability and redundancy, contributing to improved safety. Due to their simple structure and ease of control, multirotor eVTOLs are well-suited for low-altitude tourism, emergency response, and cargo transport. They are particularly advantageous in short-range missions where limited takeoff/landing space and high operational flexibility are required. However, these advantages come at the cost of lower energy and aerodynamic efficiency, limited payload capacity, and shorter operational range, making them unsuitable for long-distance missions.

In summary, multirotor eVTOLs have achieved relatively mature technological development and represent a significant pathway for rapid prototyping and initial commercial exploration within the eVTOL sector. They have demonstrated strong adaptability in urban low-altitude mobility and short-distance logistics, marking an important intermediate milestone in the engineering feasibility and practical deployment of eVTOL technologies.

#### 3.2.2. Compound-wing eVTOL

Compound-wing eVTOLs integrate characteristics of both fixed-wing and multirotor aircraft, resulting in relatively complex structural configurations. Typically, these aircraft comprise two main subsystems: a rotor system dedicated to vertical takeoff and landing, and a fixed-wing system designed to enhance lift and facilitate forward flight (). Several representative examples of compound-wing eVTOL aircraft are illustrated in.

![](https://ars.els-cdn.com/content/image/1-s2.0-S2213624X25002664-gr6.jpg)

Download: Download high-res image (103KB)

Compound-wing eVTOLs are capable of fulfilling the dual requirements of vertical takeoff/landing and efficient cruise. During VTOL operations, the rotor system generates all necessary lift, while during cruise, it can provide partial lift. This configuration significantly improves aerodynamic efficiency during forward flight (). Compared to multirotor configurations, compound-wing eVTOLs offer longer range and higher cruise speeds, effectively reducing energy consumption per unit distance. Their performance is generally balanced between that of rotorcraft and fixed-wing aircraft, making them well-suited for medium- to short-range applications such as cargo transport, emergency response, and urban mobility services. However, the increased mechanical complexity of this configuration poses challenges for weight efficiency and imposes more stringent design requirements.

In summary, compound-wing eVTOLs combine the advantages of fixed-wing and multirotor configurations. Representative models such as the Joby Aviation S4 and EHang 216 have adopted various forms of compound-wing layouts (), demonstrating the configuration’s broad applicability and promising potential in engineering implementation and industrial deployment. With continued advancements in electric propulsion and energy storage technologies, compound-wing eVTOLs are poised to become one of the mainstream configurations in future urban air mobility systems.

#### 3.2.3. Tiltrotor eVTOL

Tiltrotor eVTOL aircraft represent one of the configurations that combine the vertical takeoff and landing capabilities of helicopters with the cruise performance of fixed-wing aircraft. The fundamental design involves rotatable propulsion units—typically mounted at the wingtips or on both sides of the fuselage—that can transition between vertical and horizontal orientations. These units generate lift during vertical takeoff and landing phases and provide thrust during high-speed cruise (). Several representative tiltrotor eVTOL models are illustrated in.

![](https://ars.els-cdn.com/content/image/1-s2.0-S2213624X25002664-gr7.jpg)

Download: Download high-res image (217KB)

Tiltrotor eVTOLs require smooth aerodynamic transitions between vertical and horizontal flight modes. During vertical takeoff and landing, the rotor system supplies all lift; during the transitional tilt phase, the rotors generate both lift and thrust; and in cruise mode, the rotors are fully dedicated to producing thrust. This configuration retains the vertical lift and hovering capabilities of rotorcraft while utilizing wing-generated lift during cruise to significantly reduce energy consumption (). Additionally, this type offers high aerodynamic efficiency and superior flight speeds. Compared to multirotor and compound-wing configurations, tiltrotor eVTOLs exhibit notable advantages in terms of range and payload capacity, making them suitable for long-distance logistics, emergency response, and urban or intercity transportation. However, the most significant challenge faced by this configuration lies in the aerodynamically complex coupling during the rotor tilt transition, which complicates the reliability design and imposes high demands on tilt mechanism technology.

In summary, tiltrotor eVTOLs possess a unique capability for aerodynamic transition and efficient cruising, representing a critical pathway for advancing eVTOL performance. With continued advancements in aerodynamic design technologies, this configuration is likewise expected to emerge as one of the mainstream forms in future eVTOL development.

#### 3.2.4. Tilt-ducted fan eVTOL

Tilt-ducted fan eVTOL aircraft represent an innovative configuration that integrates ducted propellers with a tilting mechanism. The fundamental design features propellers mounted within ducts, with the entire duct and propeller assembly capable of tilting to transition between vertical and horizontal flight modes (). Several typical tilt-ducted fan eVTOL models are illustrated in.

![](https://ars.els-cdn.com/content/image/1-s2.0-S2213624X25002664-gr8.jpg)

The primary difference between tilt-ducted fan and tiltrotor eVTOLs lies in the tilt structure. The ducted design effectively enhances the thrust coefficient of the propellers at small angles of attack, improving overall system efficiency. Additionally, the duct helps suppress tip vortices to some extent, thereby achieving better aerodynamic performance and noise reduction (, ). Similar to the tiltrotor configuration, the rotor system in the tilt-ducted fan provides full lift during vertical takeoff and landing; during the tilting transition, it supplies both lift and thrust; and during cruise, it is fully dedicated to thrust generation. This design offers greater structural safety compared to tiltrotors, as the propellers are less exposed to external contact, contributing to improved flight stability and aerodynamic efficiency. With relatively high flight speeds, it is well suited for long-distance logistics, emergency response, and urban or intercity transportation. However, the ducted structure introduces additional weight, resulting in lower efficiency during hovering and vertical takeoff/landing phases. Moreover, significant aerodynamic interference occurs during duct tilting, increasing the complexity of thrust vector control.

In summary, tilt-ducted fan eVTOLs enhance thrust efficiency and flight stability but must balance structural complexity against energy utilization efficiency. Given their superior noise control capabilities, they hold significant potential for future urban air mobility applications.

### 3.3. Distributed electric propulsion design

Conventional aeronautical propulsion systems typically rely on fuel-based thermal engines, whose core operating principle involves converting chemical energy from fuel combustion into mechanical energy to generate thrust (). However, the thermal efficiency of such systems is limited by thermodynamic cycles and structural constraints; for example, typical turbofan engines convert less than 40 % of fuel energy into useful work. In contrast, electric propulsion systems directly use electrical energy as the driving source and efficiently convert electrical energy into mechanical energy through electric motors, achieving an overall energy conversion efficiency exceeding 70 % (, ). Additionally, electric propulsion technology offers significant advantages such as low noise, zero emissions, and reduced maintenance costs during operation (,,, ). Under the current global carbon neutrality and green aviation strategies, it demonstrates broad developmental prospects.

With improvements in motor power density, enhanced responsiveness of electronic control systems, and continuous advancement of high specific energy battery technologies, Distributed Electric Propulsion (DEP) has emerged as a key enabling technology for the next generation of eVTOL designs (). DEP leverages the characteristic that multiple small-to-medium power electric motor units can replace a single large propulsion unit without significant loss in overall system efficiency (). By deploying numerous modular electric motors and their corresponding propellers, DEP satisfies total thrust requirements while enhancing system flexibility and redundancy. Modularization and decoupled control of propulsion units not only improve flight safety and attitude control precision but also optimize aerodynamic configuration and energy management strategies (). Furthermore, DEP breaks the layout constraints of traditional propulsion systems, providing greater freedom and adaptability in aircraft design and supporting innovative overall configurations for novel aerial vehicles.

#### 3.3.1. System composition

The DEP system typically comprises five major subsystems: the power supply module, propulsion units, energy management and control module, power transmission structure, and auxiliary support systems. These subsystems are intricately integrated electrically and structurally, collectively forming the core power chain of the aircraft (,, ), as illustrated in.

![](https://ars.els-cdn.com/content/image/1-s2.0-S2213624X25002664-gr9.jpg)

Download: Download high-res image (91KB)

Power Supply Module: The eVTOL’s electrical energy is supplied through an integration of multiple energy sources, including solar panels, fuel cell stacks, energy storage batteries, and auxiliary generators (). Solar panels efficiently convert solar radiation into direct current electricity; fuel cells release energy via hydrogen–oxygen electrochemical reactions, offering high energy density and sustained power supply; energy storage batteries store excess power generated during peak demand periods; auxiliary generators, often small fuel-driven units, provide stable backup power or high output during takeoff phases.

Propulsion Units: The propulsion module converts electrical energy into mechanical thrust and typically consists of multiple electrically driven propulsors (), such as ducted fans or electric propellers. These propulsors generate thrust by high-speed rotation driven by electric motors, and they are critical components influencing flight performance, efficiency, and noise levels.

Energy Management and Control Module: This module is responsible for the global scheduling and distribution of energy flow within the system. Its functions include voltage stabilization, power regulation, load balancing, and system safety management (). Depending on flight states (e.g., vertical takeoff and landing, hover, cruise) and mission commands, it dynamically adjusts energy allocation among propulsion units to achieve attitude control, trajectory adjustment, and energy consumption optimization, ensuring efficient and stable operation of the electric propulsion system.

Power Transmission Structure: Serving as the mechanical linkage between the electric propulsion system and the airframe, this subsystem includes high-strength drive shafts, couplings, precision bearings, and other components that efficiently transmit torque and thrust from the propulsors to the aircraft structure, guaranteeing mechanical stability and responsive precision under various flight loads ().

Auxiliary Support Systems: These systems typically include cooling devices and protective circuits (, ). Cooling solutions may employ liquid cooling, air cooling, or phase-change materials to regulate the thermal environment of critical components such as motors and controllers in real time, preventing thermal degradation. Protection systems utilize real-time monitoring of current, voltage, and other electrical parameters to rapidly disconnect power or activate backup systems in case of overloads, short circuits, or other electrical faults, thereby enhancing overall system reliability and electrical safety.

### 3.5. Intelligent autonomous flight

One prominent development direction for eVTOL aircraft is the achievement of highly automated and intelligent autonomous flight capabilities. The technological advancement in this area currently overlaps considerably with that of autonomous UAVs. However, unlike autonomous UAVs, eVTOLs are primarily designed for manned scenarios such as urban air mobility (UAM). Their intelligent flight control systems emphasize enhancing flight autonomy and reducing pilot workload while ensuring passenger safety, ultimately realizing controlled-condition autonomous flight ().

Regarding the operational environment, eVTOLs mainly operate in low-altitude urban airspace, where they must contend with highly complex air traffic conditions, ground infrastructure constraints, and dynamic weather influences. In contrast, traditional autonomous UAVs typically operate within specific or segregated airspaces with relatively lower demands on airspace adaptability and traffic coordination. Furthermore, there is currently no standardized classification system for autonomous driving levels specifically applicable to eVTOLs (,, ).

Analogous to the classification of autonomous driving levels in automobiles, the intelligent flight levels of eVTOLs can be categorized into three tiers: VL1, VL2, and VL3, as illustrated in. VL1 corresponds to pilot-dominated operations, either onboard or remote, with the system providing auxiliary functions such as autonomous hovering and attitude stabilization, route flight, collision warning, and automatic avoidance systems (). Flight planning and execution remain the responsibility of the pilot, with the system offering recommendations, requiring continuous manual control. VL2 systems possess autonomous flight capabilities under certain conditions but necessitate human–machine collaboration, with the pilot ready to intervene as needed. Capabilities at this level include automatic takeoff and landing, collaborative route planning, autonomous route flight, and real-time autonomous obstacle avoidance (). VL3 systems achieve full autonomy, operating without pilot intervention under the supervision of remote traffic management platforms. These systems enable fully autonomous takeoff, flight, obstacle avoidance, and emergency handling, while deeply integrating with urban low-altitude traffic management systems () to realize autonomous low-altitude traffic control.

#### 3.5.1. Flight environment localization and perception

Localization and perception serve as fundamental prerequisites for enabling intelligent flight in eVTOL systems (). It refers to the process by which an aircraft, operating in complex and dynamic airspaces, acquires knowledge of its spatial position, attitude state, and external environmental features through multi-sensor data fusion and intelligent algorithmic inference. Its objective is to achieve accurate modeling and dynamic understanding of the flight environment, thereby providing reliable support for navigation, control, and mission-level decision-making.

For piloted aerial vehicles, perception and navigation systems must comply with regulatory constraints issued by aviation authorities such as the FAA, CASA, and EASA to obtain airworthiness certification. Conventional aircraft typically rely on integrated navigation systems, including Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS), Inertial Navigation Systems, VHF Omnidirectional Range (VOR), and Secondary Surveillance Radar (SSR; ). However, in low-altitude urban airspaces, GNSS—as well as VOR and SSR—may become unreliable due to signal obstruction from buildings and other infrastructure, significantly reducing the reliability of integrated navigation systems (). Unlike traditional aircraft, eVTOLs are designed to operate in such urban environments and therefore require robust positioning and environmental awareness even under GNSS-denied conditions. This has led to growing interest in Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) technologies, which enable autonomous navigation by utilizing onboard sensors (,, ).

Visual SLAM algorithms can be broadly categorized based on feature tracking methods into direct, semi-direct, and feature-based approaches. Direct methods construct photometric error models based on pixel intensity values, estimating camera poses using grayscale information from images (, ). While these methods are robust in low-texture environments and do not rely on extracted features, they are sensitive to lighting variations and generally offer lower accuracy than the other methods. Semi-direct methods aim to combine the strengths of both direct and feature-based methods (). Feature-based SLAM first identifies keypoints in the image (), followed by feature matching and solving for camera motion and map construction using techniques such as Perspective-n-Point or Bundle Adjustment (, ). Lidar-based SLAM methods can be divided into loosely coupled and tightly coupled approaches based on processing architectures (, ). Loosely coupled methods are simpler and computationally more efficient but suffer from significant IMU drift over long distances, resulting in reduced localization accuracy. Tightly coupled methods, in contrast, jointly optimize Lidar and IMU observations using factor graphs or sliding window optimization frameworks, thus providing improved precision.

Traditional SLAM approaches are primarily focused on geometric perception of the environment. However, eVTOL operations in complex low-altitude urban environments demand more than simple obstacle avoidance—they require continuous, dynamic interaction with the surrounding environment (). Therefore, enhanced semantic understanding is crucial. Emerging technologies such as semantic SLAM () and visual-based localization () show promise in addressing these challenges by integrating high-level semantic information into the localization and mapping process.

#### 3.5.2. Flight path planning and decision-making

Traditional rotorcraft UAVs typically focus on generating a feasible path through obstacle-laden environments, with common methods falling into two main categories: graph-based algorithms such as classical Dijkstra’s algorithm () and A\* (), and sampling-based algorithms such as Rapidly-Exploring Random Tree (RRT; ) and Probabilistic Roadmap (). In contrast to general UAVs, eVTOL flight path planning must account not only for obstacle avoidance and path length minimization but also for route economy and interaction strategies with ground infrastructure, buildings, and other airborne vehicles including fellow eVTOLs. To address these challenges, Wu et al. () proposed a “risk-domain-constrained” planning algorithm based on RRT, which incorporates probabilistic safety constraints to ensure statistical safety under uncertainties in both vehicle behavior and the operating environment. Additionally, they introduced a multi-vehicle fairness strategy through “permission allocation,” balancing access rights among multiple eVTOLs to generate probabilistically feasible, collision-free trajectories in dynamic environments. Recent research has also begun integrating learning-based approaches into such multi-objective constrained planning problems. Liu et al. () addressed the trade-off between energy consumption and time efficiency for eVTOLs flying in dynamic urban wind fields by proposing a deep reinforcement learning-based path planning method, and compared its performance to that of Dijkstra’s algorithm. Deniz et al. () modeled UAM operations as a multi-agent reinforcement learning problem, enabling eVTOLs to dynamically adjust their flight altitude across multi-layered airspace. Their method aims to simultaneously minimize ground-level noise and maintain inter-aircraft separation. Results show that agents learned to balance altitude elevation for noise reduction with the need to avoid aerial congestion.

In summary, while traditional path planning techniques provide foundational capabilities for eVTOL trajectory generation, they are insufficient for the multifaceted constraints of complex UAM environments. Emerging approaches are evolving toward the integration of risk modeling, fairness strategies, and deep reinforcement learning, significantly enhancing path safety, operational efficiency, and the decision-making autonomy of eVTOLs in multi-agent, dynamic, and urban low-altitude airspace scenarios.

#### 3.5.3. Flight trajectory optimization and control

The task of the trajectory optimization module is to generate smooth, dynamically feasible, collision-free, and efficient trajectories based on reference waypoints provided by the path planning algorithm (). Wang et al. () developed a convex optimization-based trajectory planning framework applicable to the full flight envelope of eVTOLs, including cruise, descent, and landing phases. This framework enables real-time optimization under various operational constraints. Comparative simulations against pseudospectral optimal control methods demonstrated the advantages of the convex approach in both accuracy and computational efficiency.

Given the diversity in dynamic characteristics among different eVTOL configurations, control law design varies significantly (,, ). For multirotor eVTOLs, the control algorithms () are similar to those used in multirotor UAVs; however, differences in size and payload may lead to variations in control effectiveness. Compound-wing and tiltrotor eVTOLs (, ), which integrate the agility of multirotors with the endurance of fixed-wing aircraft, are capable of vertical take-off and landing as well as efficient forward cruise. These configurations require advanced control strategies to ensure stable operations across both flight modes and during transition phases. Recent research has addressed the unsteady aerodynamic challenges during mode transitions through methods such as model predictive control, state observers, and learning-based controllers. Yang et al. () proposed a unified control law based on H∞ control theory, applicable to both rotorcraft and fixed-wing flight regimes, while mode transitions are managed via a typical feedforward tilt-rotor scheduling strategy.

Trajectory optimization for eVTOLs is evolving from simplified hard-constraint-based models toward soft-constraint-based approaches capable of handling complex urban environments, thereby enhancing trajectory safety and flexibility. On the control side, different eVTOL configurations necessitate tailored control strategies: traditional methods can be adapted for multirotors, while compound-wing and tiltrotor types require robust multimodal control. Advanced techniques such as robust control and model predictive control are increasingly applied to ensure accurate control under transition states and complex aerodynamic conditions, thereby continuously advancing the autonomous flight capabilities of eVTOLs.

The service network serves as the core integrated regulatory platform for the low-altitude economy, primarily comprising subsystems such as the low-altitude surveillance system, flight service system, and flight management and control system (). ### 3.7. Airworthiness and safety

As an emerging industry, eVTOL safety and airworthiness have become one of the core factors driving its large-scale development. Ensuring safe operations in complex urban airspace environments has become a focal concern for both the industry and regulatory authorities (, ). Unlike conventional aircraft, eVTOLs exhibit significant differences and innovations in propulsion systems, flight control logic, structural layout, and operational scenarios. Consequently, traditional aviation airworthiness standards cannot be directly applied, necessitating the development of safety assessment models and airworthiness certification mechanisms tailored to the unique characteristics of eVTOLs.

Currently, although there are certain differences among countries in aircraft airworthiness certification, the overall procedural framework is highly similar, fundamentally adhering to the principle of “safety-objective-based airworthiness certification” (). While eVTOLs represent a new class of aircraft integrating electric propulsion, intelligent control, and automated operation—embodying advanced future technologies—they remain fundamentally categorized as aircraft. Therefore, they must be managed and certified within the civil aviation airworthiness regulatory frameworks of respective countries. Certification authorities generally maintain the traditional certification logic within their overall procedural frameworks but have introduced corresponding adjustments and supplements in specific technical requirements, testing and validation criteria, and interpretation of applicable provisions based on eVTOL technical features. #### 3.7.1. Airworthiness certification process

Airworthiness certification is the first step toward the commercialization of low-altitude eVTOL aircraft. Overall, the mainstream airworthiness certification process can be divided into three stages ():

The Type Certificate (TC) forms the foundational step of airworthiness certification. Its core objective is to verify whether the overall aircraft design complies with airworthiness regulations and environmental standards. The Production Certificate (PC) focuses on quality assurance during the mass production phase. Compared to the TC’s emphasis on “design validation,” the PC audit highlights the controllability and continuity of “manufacturing consistency.” The PC process centers on a systematic evaluation of the manufacturer’s quality management system, covering multiple dimensions such as design data control, process flow management, inspection and testing systems, supply chain quality control, and non-conformance handling mechanisms. The Airworthiness Certificate (AC) assesses the airworthiness status of a specific individual aircraft, ensuring its actual capability for safe flight. The AC evaluation targets a single completed product and includes review of technical documentation completeness, compliance with airworthiness directives, equipment installation records, and maintenance release documents. A key focus is verifying whether the aircraft meets the airworthiness baseline stipulated by the TC prior to delivery. For eVTOLs, AC evaluation additionally requires particular attention to high-risk components such as the propulsion battery system, flight control software, and safety redundancy systems, ensuring flight safety is not compromised by failure of critical systems.

On this basis, the Civil Aviation Administration of China (CAAC) has established a four-tier certification system—TC, PC, AC, and Operating Certificate (OC)—forming a complete technical review chain. The OC, as the final step in the airworthiness certification system, constitutes the legal foundation and prerequisite for commercial operation of the aircraft. According to civil aviation authorities’ requirements, obtaining the OC necessitates meeting four core conditions: 1) A clear and fully functional organizational structure capable of effectively supporting operational management and safety decision-making; 2) Availability of certified airworthy aircraft and the necessary technical equipment for operation; 3) Establishment of comprehensive operational control systems, maintenance management procedures, and personnel qualification processes; 4) Legal acquisition of relevant civil aviation transport operating licenses or airspace operation rights. The issuance of the OC marks the formal transition of eVTOL operations from the experimental verification phase to commercial application, granting legal status and operational permission to conduct compensated transportation services.

NASA’s Unmanned Aircraft System Traffic Management (UTM) program has conducted pilot demonstrations at multiple sites to verify data sharing and conflict detection between low-altitude aircraft and air traffic management systems, providing prototype validation for the future deployment of UAM operations.

### 4.2. Application market development trends

In the commercialization trajectory of eVTOLs, passenger transport is widely regarded as the ultimate developmental direction and core application scenario. It is anticipated that in the initial phase, eVTOLs will first be deployed in low-risk, low-complexity domains such as cargo logistics and urban public services, thereby facilitating the accumulation of operational experience and the enhancement of market acceptance. With the continuous improvement of battery energy density and system integration efficiency, the range capabilities of eVTOLs are expected to be significantly extended, gradually expanding their applications from urban air mobility (UAM) to regional air mobility (RAM) and supporting broader demands for aerial transportation.

In terms of airworthiness certification and industrial ecosystem development, the relevant regulatory frameworks have gradually become more defined, and the certification processes for various eVTOL models are advancing in an orderly fashion. The industrial ecosystem is accelerating its formation, and between 2025 and 2026, multiple leading enterprises are expected to complete airworthiness certification of their flagship models and achieve initial market deployment (). Meanwhile, the manufacturing sector is entering a phase of consolidation, with increasing market concentration. Given the long cycles, high capital requirements, and intensive technological inputs associated with eVTOL research, certification, and commercialization, the industry structure will increasingly concentrate around enterprises with strong financial capacity and substantial technological reserves. Firms that possess robust financing ability and autonomous R&D capabilities are likely to become the dominant players in future competition.

More importantly, the market development of eVTOLs should not be confined to the commercialization of an isolated transport modality but rather be situated within the broader framework of Urban Air Mobility (UAM) development strategies. As a crucial component of future integrated urban transportation systems, UAM requires not only seamless information exchange and operational coordination with existing ground transportation but also the establishment of a comprehensive support ecosystem encompassing infrastructure (e.g., vertiports, air corridors), traffic flow management (e.g., airspace resource allocation, route optimization), and regulatory and policy frameworks (e.g., airworthiness standards, air traffic control, insurance, and liability definition). Looking ahead, the market expansion of eVTOLs is expected to evolve from localized pilot applications to networked large-scale operations, with developmental pathways exhibiting significant synergy and coupling potential with intelligent connected vehicles and urban smart transportation systems.

## 5\. Conclusion

Against the strategic backdrop of the rapid development of the low-altitude economy, this paper begins with the developmental history of eVTOLs, systematically reviewing their technological evolution and, based on in-depth research, conducting a focused analysis of their core technological systems. The article discusses seven key technologies, namely overall aerodynamic configuration design, distributed electric propulsion design, long-endurance batteries, intelligent autonomous flight, digital low-altitude traffic management systems, and safety and airworthiness. Building upon this, the paper further explores the future development trends of these critical technologies and, in conjunction with the current industry status, analyzes the market expansion pathways and evolutionary directions of eVTOLs in urban air mobility and broader application scenarios.

In summary, as a vital carrier for the strategic implementation of the low-altitude economy, eVTOLs are currently at a pivotal stage transitioning from technological validation to commercial application. Their development not only poses new challenges to the traditional aerospace engineering domain but also brings profound impacts to airspace management, regulatory frameworks, and the construction of industrial ecosystems.
