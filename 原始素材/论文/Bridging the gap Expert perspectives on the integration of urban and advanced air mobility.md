## 1\. Introduction

Urban Air Mobility (UAM) and Advanced Air Mobility (AAM) are set to revolutionize transportation by providing innovative methods for moving people and goods through the air. The industry is progressing rapidly, driven by advancements in autonomous systems, electric propulsion, and unmanned aerial vehicles. However, this growth introduces various technical, regulatory, and operational challenges that must be addressed before these systems can be safely and effectively integrated into existing transportation networks. By capturing the insights and concerns of those at the forefront of this transformation, this paper highlights the barriers and the essential steps required to address them. Understanding these perspectives is crucial for informing policy decisions, technological advancements, and business strategies that will shape the future of air mobility.

### 1.1. Background

UAM and AAM are often considered synonymous. UAM focuses on transporting passengers or cargo within densely populated urban areas and is vital to the broader AAM concept (). The systems involved in UAM/AAM aim to deliver sustainable, safe, affordable, and accessible transportation solutions for cargo and passenger needs (). Regardless of their urban focus, the primary objective remains the same. These systems promise significant advantages, including reduced road congestion, enhanced emergency response capabilities, faster cargo delivery times, and the potential for economic and technological growth (; ). With the United States expected to grow by 70 million people over the next 30 years (), these systems are becoming an increasingly feasible and practical option.

## 2\. Literature review

### 2.1. Public willingness/economic viability

For successful UAM/AAM operations, the public must accept the technology in urban and rural areas (). A major obstacle to adoption is a lack of public trust, often caused by limited knowledge about the technology, highlighting the importance of establishing initial trust in the system (; ). Vehicles must be financially viable for both consumers and operators. Estimates suggest that operational costs will be significantly higher than those of traditional road vehicles for cargo transport (). However, UAM/AAM provides a financially viable option for short-distance passenger travel in crowded cities ().

identify key factors influencing public adoption of UAM/AAM technologies, including income, age, gender, education, and commute length. Rideshare users are particularly receptive, a trend supported by, who found that younger generations are more accepting of such technologies. UAM has promising applications in airport transport, potentially alleviating congestion, as evidenced by the current use of helicopters at major terminals (). estimate that UAM could capture 3.6% of the airport transport market at Los Angeles International Airport, reflecting public interest in congested areas. The European Union Aviation Safety Agency notes that faster response times and lower costs attract interest in UAM and AAM, but safety, security, and noise concerns deter some users. Despite this, acceptance is growing due to their potential benefits, though infrastructure and airspace challenges remain.

### 2.2. Infrastructure/airspace

UAM/AAM aircraft are expected to outnumber commercial aircraft operating within the national airspace system (). This projected increase in air traffic density creates challenges for maintaining safe separation between two UAM aircraft and between a UAM and crewed aircraft. The resulting congestion will require a significant restructuring of the airspace system to ensure seamless and safe integration (; ). Research on airspace design, dedicated corridors, and operational impacts continues to expand. For example, have identified corridors around Orlando International Airport that could support up to 22 aircraft operating simultaneously without disrupting commercial air traffic. This development marks a promising step toward integrating operations in high-traffic areas.

Additionally, urban infrastructure must be re-evaluated. Although this poses challenges, it could ultimately lower infrastructure costs and foster economic growth over time (;; ). Vertiports are emerging as a practical infrastructure solution. The National Aeronautics and Space Administration recommends categorizing landing areas into three types: vertihubs, vertiports, and vertistops, based on their size and operational needs. The success of deploying this infrastructure will heavily rely on technological advancements and public acceptance.

### 2.3. Technology

Aircraft, airspace, operations, infrastructure, and community are essential for the success (). Improved technology is needed to address current automation gaps. Navigation systems, such as the Global Navigation Satellite System, face reliability issues in urban settings, which could necessitate a return to ground-based navigation methods (;; ). Communication systems must provide continuous air-to-ground and air-to-air connectivity, necessitating a reliable infrastructure (). While 5G could offer urban communication, priority must be given to UAM/AAM vehicles (; ). High-altitude platforms and satellite communications offer cost-effective options, although further testing is necessary (). Incorporating artificial intelligence is crucial for improving decision-making in urban navigation (; ). These advancements enhance safety but also raise the need for robust cybersecurity measures.

### 2.4. Cybersecurity/safety

Safety and cybersecurity should be considered together during development. Operational safety risk assessments, such as those conducted by, help identify high-risk areas in operations based on past events. Even after implementation, this ongoing effort will be necessary to mitigate emerging risks. Systems like Detect and Avoid (DAA) will be critical for reducing collision risks between vehicles (). Vehicle-to-vehicle communication systems have shown promise in DAA efforts (). Current research by,, and will contribute to improving and establishing the requirements for a DAA system. Enhanced cybersecurity measures must be implemented to ensure the reliability and safety of these systems. Current UAM/AAM operations have many cybersecurity vulnerabilities (). Intentionally disrupting communications poses a serious safety threat that has yet to be addressed. Advanced security technologies, such as blockchain measures, offer a way to protect data transfer and communication securely (). AI-based safety analyzers help detect cybersecurity threats in real time by identifying system anomalies (). Research on improved risk assessment, incident response, system resilience, and standards remains necessary ().

### 2.5. Regulatory

Current policies and regulations are not suited for the autonomy required for widespread AAM operations. Enhancements in federal, state, and local laws will be necessary, involving all key stakeholders, including policymakers, urban planners, and the public (). Modifications to the regulatory framework complicate implementation (; ). At a minimum, regulations concerning operations beyond visual line of sight and over people and property must be established beyond 14 CFR 107. Strengthening rules can help ensure a safe implementation ().

### 2.6. Current study

Conducting qualitative expert interviews offers a deep understanding of realistic expectations and requirements for implementing UAM and AAM. Research in this area has provided valuable background information and guiding principles for addressing various challenges. The exploration of UAM/AAM has dramatically increased over the past decade. In 2015, only six scholarly publications focused on UAM/AAM, but by 2021, that number had risen to 259 (). Current research can be categorized mainly into market, operations, and aircraft technology (). Despite rapid progress in this field, there is a lack of direct input from experts. Based on expert interview input, this research offers essential insights into the barriers and challenges encountered with implementing and sustaining UAM/AAM.

## 3\. Methods

This study was based on qualitative interviews with professionals and stakeholders in the UAM/AAM sectors. Given the rapidly evolving nature of these industries, qualitative research is particularly useful for capturing expert insights, identifying emerging challenges, and understanding the nuances of industry concerns that may not yet be reflected in regulatory frameworks or technical literature. Qualitative research also enables rich, thick descriptions from participants that offer meaningful insights into individuals involved in implementing UAM/AAM.

### 3.1. Research method and design

The study follows a qualitative exploratory research design commonly used in emerging industries where standardized data is limited. The goal was to gather firsthand perspectives from individuals actively developing, regulating, and implementing UAM and AAM technologies. Using open-ended questions and discussions allowed participants to express their thoughts freely, highlighting well-established concerns and overlooked challenges.

### 3.2. Participants, sampling, and groups

Participants were recruited via a purposive sampling process. Through this process, the research team identified experts in each field listed in. Purposive sampling was employed in this study due to the unique and novel characteristics of the emerging UAM/AAM industry. The research team created the three fields to ensure representation of the major fields that may likely influence UAM/AAM development and deployment. Participants received an email from the research team describing the purpose of the study and seeking their willingness to participate in an interview.

### 3.3. Analysis, coding, and validation

A thematic analysis approach was used to analyze the data. This process comprised several steps: familiarizing ourselves with the data, an initial coding assessment, code review, theme development, and conversations across the research team as themes emerged. This process occurred while the interviews were ongoing to identify data saturation, the point at which no new themes emerged in the field or themes began to recur.

From a technical perspective, the audio files were listened to for clarity and accuracy. Following this, the audio files were transcribed following the interviews using Google Notebook LM. To verify the accuracy of the transcription, the transcribed files were compared against the audio recordings. Transcripts were then reviewed, and key themes were identified based on recurring concerns, insights, and areas of debate. In addition to human analysis and inspection, Google Notebook LM was used to help assess and identify themes and patterns within the transcriptions. The analysis focused on identifying common threads across different stakeholder groups and recognizing points of divergence where different industry sectors held conflicting perspectives. This helped structure the findings in a way that reflects both consensus and unresolved tensions within the field.

### 3.4. Materials, stimuli, and ethical considerations

Interviews were constructed using a semi-structured interview questionnaire. Each group had 8-12 questions specific to their implementation period. All interviews were audio recorded and then transcribed for data analysis as described in the prior section. Prior to the start of data collection, the researchers submitted and received an approved IRB protocol. The protocol outlined the proper procedures for conducting the interviews, obtaining informed consent, and ensuring the confidentiality aspects of handling, analyzing, and storing the data.

### 4.1. Detect and avoid systems and airspace management

Experts stressed the importance of integrating UAM and AAM into current systems and the challenges in creating effective Unmanned Traffic Management (UTM). While NASA has been developing air corridor models, applying them in real-world scenarios presents several challenges. Issues include integrating data to understand disruption impacts better. Moreover, there is still uncertainty about the regulatory framework for UTM systems. Experts note that the current air traffic control infrastructure is not well-equipped to handle large volumes of low-altitude flights, emphasizing the need for new solutions. Incorporating these aircraft into the airspace is complex without clear guidelines or investments in advanced UTM technology.

These expert concerns are consistent with prior UTM research emphasizing that conventional air traffic control architectures were not designed for dense, low-altitude operations and that scalable UAS integration requires new distributed traffic management concepts incorporating dynamic airspace configuration, automated conflict management, and enhanced communication, navigation, and surveillance capabilities (; ). Existing studies similarly identify the need for integrating real-world operational variables such as weather impacts, traffic congestion, and performance monitoring metrics to support operational scalability and safety validation (). Furthermore, broader analyses of future airspace systems highlight that traditional controller-centric frameworks may not scale effectively to high-volume urban operations, reinforcing the need for new low-altitude management paradigms and distributed decision-support architectures (; ).

> “NASA has been developing air corridor models, but real-world application remains a concern. We need to include real-world data, including as weather conditions and airspace delays, into these algorithms.”

> “The regulatory pathway for UTM systems is currently uncertain. The FAA (Federal Aviation Administration) is attempting to incorporate them, but current ATC (Air Traffic Control) infrastructure is not built to manage low-altitude, high-volume operations like these.”

> “The purpose of our conventional ATC systems was to track aircraft at high altitudes. New tracking systems are required for urban operations at low altitudes.”

There is increasing debate over who should be responsible for air traffic management: human pilots, remote operators, or automated systems. Current aviation models place full responsibility for collision avoidance on pilots; experts believe a new accountability framework is necessary for highly automated aircraft. Although pilots and remote operators should ultimately make avoidance decisions, automated traffic management systems must take on a larger role in conflict detection and maintaining separation. Relying solely on human operators in busy airspace is impractical; thus, automated systems should primarily handle conflict detection and execute avoidance maneuvers. A clear hierarchy of accountability must be established, delineating duties among pilots, remote operators, and automation systems. Currently, regulations are ambiguous on this balance, creating uncertainty for regulators and operators. Without explicit guidelines, assigning liability after an incident will be challenging.

These concerns align with longstanding human−automation interaction research demonstrating that increasing levels of automation shift operator roles from direct control toward supervisory control, while simultaneously creating ambiguity in authority, responsibility, and accountability allocation (; ). Empirical studies show that higher stages and levels of automation alter workload distribution, situation awareness, and trust, and can degrade performance if responsibility boundaries are not explicitly defined (; ). Function allocation research further emphasizes that distributing authority and responsibility across human and autonomous agents requires careful analysis of interdependencies, monitoring requirements, and intervention capabilities to prevent “clumsy automation” or responsibility gaps (; ). In increasingly autonomous aviation contexts, formal responsibility reasoning frameworks have similarly highlighted the risk of responsibility diffusion and the need to explicitly model prospective and retrospective accountability in human−agent collectives (). These findings reinforce expert assertions that scalable UTM and DAA architectures must define explicit responsibility hierarchies when automation assumes primary conflict detection and separation functions.

> “It is unclear who should be in charge of air traffic management. Everyone currently expects that pilots and air traffic controllers handle things, but in a future where automation plays a larger role, there must be explicit accountability for traffic separation.”

DAA systems are essential for safe operations. Experts emphasize that DAA should focus on collision avoidance, rather than broad airspace awareness. To prevent onboard or remote pilots from being overwhelmed with information, these systems should focus on identifying and reacting to potential collision threats within a critical zone around the aircraft. This targeted approach allows pilots or automation to respond quickly to immediate hazards, avoiding non-essential data. DAA technology currently employs onboard sensors (like radar and electro-optical/infrared cameras) and ground-based systems (including ADS-B). Experts questioned whether these technologies are mature enough to handle the complexities of urban airspace. The effectiveness of DAA primarily depends on its ability to deliver clear, specific instructions upon detecting a collision threat, rather than leaving avoidance decisions to pilot discretion.

These expert assertions are consistent with a substantial body of research on alert design and information management in high-reliability systems. Studies of collision avoidance and cockpit alerting systems have repeatedly shown that excessive or poorly filtered alerts can lead to alarm fatigue, degraded trust, and reduced compliance, particularly in time-critical environments (; ). In Traffic Collision Avoidance System (TCAS) development, balancing early detection with minimization of nuisance alerts was identified as a central design challenge, as false or low-relevance advisories negatively affect pilot response reliability and long-term trust in automation (). Research further demonstrates that systems which present prescriptive, context-specific resolution advisories, rather than raw traffic data requiring independent pilot interpretation, improve response consistency under time pressure ().

From a cognitive engineering perspective, ecological interface design research emphasizes that interfaces in complex sociotechnical systems should prioritize information that reflects functional constraints relevant to immediate task demands, thereby reducing cognitive load and supporting adaptive problem solving (). Work Domain Analysis applied to automated collision detection has similarly shown that effective alerting displays must represent collision dynamics in a manner that maps directly to system constraints and threat geometry, rather than presenting unstructured traffic data (). These findings support expert recommendations that DAA systems concentrate on clearly defined collision prediction zones and deliver actionable guidance within a critical conflict envelope, rather than broad situational awareness displays that risk overwhelming onboard or remote pilots.

Moreover, evaluations of conflict detection and resolution systems indicate that in high-density airspace environments, scalable safety performance depends on filtering and prioritizing imminent threats while suppressing low-probability or non-actionable alerts to prevent unnecessary maneuvering and automation disengagement (; ). Collectively, prior research reinforces the importance of targeted, context-sensitive alerting architectures in DAA systems to maintain both operational safety and human-automation trust in increasingly complex urban airspace environments.

> “Today, detect and avoid operates practically independently. You have a system that is only looking for a disagreement and does not provide much context. There must also be the creation of technologies that absorb and contextualize that information within the UTM infrastructure."

> "I feel that the DAA’s main purpose should be to detect potential collision impact locations in the crucial zone surrounding the aircraft. It should alert to intruders in the crucial collision prediction area, as established by the algorithms input into the DAA equipment."

These observations reflect broader research indicating that standalone collision avoidance systems are insufficient for high-density low-altitude operations and must be integrated within distributed traffic management architectures that combine onboard sensing with networked information exchange and cooperative surveillance. Studies of emerging UTM and U-Space frameworks emphasize that scalable urban operations require contextualized traffic information and layered conflict management capabilities rather than isolated aircraft-centric alerting logic (;; ).

A barrier to automation is the absence of reliable DAA standards and standardized regulations for low-altitude operations. Current RTCA standards do not cover operations below 1000 feet or the unique challenges of UAM, such as navigating between buildings or avoiding unexpected obstacles. Until these standards are updated, operations may be restricted to a “one pilot, one aircraft” model, rather than allowing a single remote operator to oversee multiple vehicles. Additionally, conspicuity—ensuring that aircraft are visible and trackable by air traffic management and other aircraft—is critical for safety. Experts concur that robust DAA systems and enhanced conspicuity are essential to support greater automation. Experts believe DAA technology is progressing in areas such as onboard radar, ground-based surveillance, and AI-driven conflict resolution. Despite these significant improvements, the systems require further refinement before being widely deployed.

Prior research similarly identifies unresolved regulatory and infrastructure challenges as primary barriers to scaling UAM operations, particularly in very-low-level airspace where surveillance coverage, communication reliability, and standardized performance requirements remain under development. Studies of CNS infrastructure and urban airspace architectures highlight that conspicuity and interoperable surveillance are foundational enablers for transitioning toward supervisory control models in which a single operator may oversee multiple vehicles safely (;; ). These findings reinforce expert assessments that technological progress alone is insufficient without concurrent development of harmonized standards and integrated infrastructure capable of supporting dense mixed-traffic environments.

> “ *The standards exist, and the technology is being developed. The only gap that exists in standards is the technical void below 1000 ft. But that, I would argue, is not necessarily an autonomy thing. That is an ‘aviation at the large’ case, and the industry is also looking at closing the gap on where the standards have been lacking.*”

> “The key enablers here are conspicuity and detection and avoidance systems. The moment that we can implement very reliable detection and avoidance systems, sense and avoid systems, plus good conspicuity, then we can start talking about one pilot managing several vehicles in cases of more advanced automation.”

Experts emphasize that DAA should be seen not only as a tool for remotely piloted aircraft but also as a comprehensive enhancement to aviation safety. These systems will support both crewed and uncrewed aircraft, contributing to safer airspace for all users. However, awareness of the maturity of these technologies remains limited, causing some stakeholders to perceive them as experimental rather than vital components of aviation infrastructure.

These observations align with prior research indicating that low-altitude integration challenges below 1000 ft are primarily linked to infrastructure standardization, surveillance coverage, and system interoperability rather than autonomy capability alone. Studies of UTM and U-Space architectures emphasize conspicuity, cooperative surveillance, and integrated CNS ecosystems as foundational enablers for scalable operations involving both crewed and uncrewed aircraft (;; ). Furthermore, recent analyses highlight that framing DAA as a shared safety layer across mixed-traffic environments supports broader aviation safety objectives while facilitating transitions toward supervised multi-vehicle operations (; ).

> “Then we talk about very low or low density scenario, then we can imagine that, yes, we will have, as you know, one pilot, controlling the drone because we need to be conservative. We know safety first. So for me it’s key that we identify those enablers. …. In my role, I am the co-chair of one ICAO group working on a vision for advancing mobility, and we are really struggling to identify what the key enablers are that will allow us to, you know, to progress to a bit more sophisticated scenarios. So it’s not an easy discussion to be honest.”

> “ *It is very often that we say these types of operations are not ready because the technology is not available. My response is: the industry is working on these technologies. It will help advanced remotely piloted supervised operations but these same technologies could be used by crewed operations to increase their safety.* …. *Technology is advancing, and it’s at a state where you know we need to come up and modernize the system.*”

Experts agree that quick responses are crucial in congested urban areas to maintain safe separation. While there may be expert bias due to competing interests, the respondents had strong consensus that automated systems are more reliable than human operators for executing avoidance maneuvers. Many suggest that autonomous responses to DAA alerts should be prioritized, as computers can process and perform maneuvers faster and more accurately. One participant pointed out that current traffic collision systems on commercial aircraft still require pilots to disengage automation and manually intervene for avoidance. However, this is mainly true for older systems, as newer aircraft often do not require pilots to disengage autopilot, depending on the aircraft and operator. Manual collision avoidance systems are seen as a limitation, especially since future operations will need tighter integration between DAA and flight automation to reduce reaction times.

These observations align with existing research showing that scalable low-altitude operations depend on integrating DAA capabilities with flight automation and cooperative traffic management infrastructure rather than relying solely on pilot-initiated responses. Prior studies similarly identify reliable DAA performance, conspicuity, and clearly defined human–automation authority allocation as key enablers for transitioning toward supervised multi-vehicle operations in mixed crewed and uncrewed airspace (;; ).

> “If the autopilot was connected, it would probably perform just as well as the pilot, but normal operating procedures now require pilots to disengage the autopilot and manually commence avoidance maneuvers. This is something that must alter for highly automated aircraft.”

Finally, experts expressed concerns that the U.S. is falling behind in airspace modernization compared to other nations. Many countries are upgrading their communication, navigation, and surveillance (CNS) infrastructure to accommodate UAM and AAM operations. Meanwhile, the slow regulatory pace has created uncertainties about integrating new technologies. Experts recommend shifting focus toward deploying existing solutions rather than pursuing further research. Many DAA systems are already in advanced development stages, but regulatory challenges and interoperability issues have delayed their adoption.

These observations are consistent with existing UAM and UTM research, indicating that technological capability is advancing faster than regulatory harmonization and infrastructure modernization, particularly in CNS systems required for scalable low-altitude operations. Comparative analyses of U-Space and international ATM modernization efforts similarly highlight differences in implementation pace across regions and emphasize that interoperability standards and certification pathways, rather than technical feasibility alone, remain primary barriers to operational deployment (;; ).

> “ *The industry is not viewing this as a science project. …. They view these systems as also enhancing the safety of aviation. These detect and avoid systems are nothing more than the next generation of PCAP systems.* …. *It shouldn’t take 20 years to make this happen when the technology is here. …. We no longer can operate under that paradigm. …. Look at what’s happening in China. Look at what’s happening in Korea, right? Look at what’s happening in Europe as part of their ATM modernization roadmap. I think that is not to say that those jurisdictions are perfect, but the fact that they are more modernizing their airspace is central to this detect and avoid piece.”*

### 4.2. Automation, remote piloting, and the role of AI

Automation is viewed as a crucial enabler for the future, allowing aircraft to operate more efficiently while decreasing the need for constant human intervention. Experts emphasized that automation should extend beyond basic flight control to include 4D trajectory management, which integrates spatial positioning with time-based coordination. 4D trajectory-based operations have been widely proposed in next-generation air traffic management systems as a means of coordinating aircraft movements across both spatial and temporal dimensions while optimizing airspace capacity and maintaining safe separation between vehicles (). This approach aligns with traditional air traffic management methods but requires new digital infrastructure to enable real-time data sharing between operators, regulators, and aircraft systems. Emerging traffic management frameworks therefore rely on integrated information architectures capable of aggregating and distributing operational data, such as traffic information, weather updates, and airspace constraints, to both automated systems and human operators to maintain situational awareness and support conflict mitigation in increasingly complex airspace environments ().

> *“For me what really makes sense is that we use full advantage of all the technology behind all these innovative vehicles that they can do things that we cannot do …… and that’s why we need to think about how we can implement higher levels of automation.*”

Automation is vital in traffic sequencing, particularly in vertiport environments where multiple aircraft may arrive and depart simultaneously. Research on 4D trajectory optimization and dynamic air traffic management demonstrates that automated trajectory planning can maximize airspace throughput while maintaining probabilistic collision avoidance guarantees, particularly in high-density and time-constrained environments (; ). Experts recommend that operators connect to an automated traffic management system that coordinates aircraft movements, assigns landing priorities, and maintains proper spacing. Emerging UTM architectures similarly emphasize distributed information services and centralized network management to enable real-time coordination and deconfliction across multiple vehicles operating in shared low-altitude airspace (). Autonomy does not eliminate humans; instead, a human-in-the-loop approach is preferred. Human–autonomy teaming research consistently shows that supervisory control models, where operators plan, monitor, and intervene rather than manually execute low-level control, support higher workload scalability while preserving situation awareness and trust calibration (; ). In this model, remote pilots supervise automated systems and intervene when necessary. Automation can efficiently handle routine tasks such as navigation and collision avoidance, allowing humans to focus on strategic decision-making. Supervisory control literature indicates that reallocating routine control tasks to automation reduces cognitive burden and enables operators to manage multiple vehicles more effectively, provided transparency and intervention mechanisms are preserved (). Remote pilots managing multiple aircraft cannot manually respond to every avoidance alert in real time, so automation takes over collision avoidance, enabling pilots to concentrate on higher-level monitoring and decision-making. This task reallocation aligns with established levels-of-automation frameworks, which recommend higher automation for time-critical response functions while retaining human oversight for strategic and ethical decisions ().

> “Autonomy in the UAS should refer to the autonomy of not only landing but also linking to a central air traffic control system. That is the direction we are heading towards, with NASA leading the way.”

> “For midterm and mature operations, remote pilots will require detect and avoid capabilities in conjunction with autopilot. Given the potential for congested airspace, automation should handle routine maneuvers to guarantee safe separation.”

Participants believe remote pilots need advanced tools as operations expand, including fully integrated DAA systems with autopilot capabilities. Studies on collision-free trajectory computation show that integrated motion-planning algorithms can jointly optimize flight efficiency and separation constraints, particularly when coupled with centralized or distributed network managers (). Experts emphasize that, although automation will optimize airspace use, it must be paired with reliable DAA solutions to ensure safety in complex environments.

Human factors research further underscores that automation reliability and transparency are critical for maintaining operator trust and preventing overreliance or complacency in supervisory roles (). Without dependable detection technology, and more sophistication on the avoidance algorithm side for complex environments like terminal areas, operations may be limited to low-density areas with strict pilot oversight, restricting scalability. The ability to monitor automated decisions and override them is vital for safety and regulatory compliance. Supervisory control models consistently emphasize the importance of bi-directional communication and operator-directed interfaces to support effective intervention and recovery from automation failures (). AI-driven systems analyze patterns in air traffic, weather conditions, and past flight data to detect potential hazards proactively.

Intelligent decision-support systems using neural networks and probabilistic risk models have demonstrated potential to enhance collaborative decision-making and predictive hazard detection in aviation contexts (). AI also enhances return-to-mission capabilities, allowing aircraft to adjust routes if unexpected obstacles arise autonomously. Experts warn that AI should only be implemented after thorough testing and human oversight, as these systems can be brittle and fail in novel situations. Research on automation brittleness and trust calibration shows that insufficient transparency or misaligned expectations between human operators and intelligent systems can degrade performance, particularly in edge-case scenarios (). Additionally, operators require specialized training to understand AI functions and intervene when necessary to prevent errors. Many experts believe AI and machine learning will significantly improve capabilities, especially predictive collision avoidance.

> “ *There is a remote pilot that is leveraging increased levels of automation and situational awareness that supports that automation to ensure safety and decision-making. We’re not sending a drone that has, you know, a level five fully autonomous system into the air. We’re sending an aircraft into the air that has a human being in the loop providing supervision over a fixed route network, which is very deterministic following standards.”*

> “We need to understand how humans engage with the system. What level of automation should they have, and how much control should pilots or remote operators retain?”

Situational awareness is crucial for remote pilots, combining real-time data feeds, predictive analytics, and automation assistance, leading experts to discuss “airspace sanitization.” Before an aircraft enters the landing phase, the surrounding airspace must be cleared of obstacles and conflicting traffic. UTM information service architectures highlight the importance of spatially and temporally correlated data layers to support pre-entry deconfliction and dynamic airspace constraint management (). Coordination among automated systems, remote pilots, and air traffic managers is vital to prevent last-minute conflicts. Remote pilots also need advanced vision systems to operate aircraft safely without direct sight. Experts have proposed a comprehensive 360-degree awareness system for real-time aircraft environment visualization. Visualization and ecological interface design research demonstrates that augmented spatial displays improve operator comprehension of complex traffic geometries and system intentions, particularly in multi-vehicle supervisory contexts (). These systems help pilots identify safe landing zones and navigation routes, but are not necessarily part of the DAA framework. In urban areas, where vertiports may be on rooftops or confined spaces, precise situational awareness is essential for safe landings. ### 4.3. Communication, data sharing, and cybersecurity

Experts emphasize that cybersecurity is an increasing threat, raising concerns about the vulnerability of remotely operated and autonomous aircraft to hacking and unauthorized control. One participant noted that “any vehicle operated remotely or autonomously could be susceptible to unlawful seizure.” This concern is strongly supported in the cybersecurity literature, which identifies unmanned aircraft as cyber-physical systems that rely heavily on wireless communication links and onboard software, making them susceptible to unauthorized access, manipulation of flight controls, and data interception if adequate security mechanisms are not implemented (). Despite some manufacturers claiming their systems are ‘unhackable,’ this belief is criticized as dangerously naive. Experts warn that attackers could mimic control signals, compromising aircraft security without the operator's knowledge. Research on UAV cybersecurity confirms that attackers may exploit weaknesses in wireless communication channels to intercept transmissions, conduct spoofing attacks, or inject malicious commands, potentially allowing adversaries to manipulate aircraft behavior or access sensitive flight data (; ). Addressing these cybersecurity threats is vital for the industry's future. Experts recommend regular penetration testing and continuous in-flight system monitoring to mitigate these risks and detect malicious activity. The literature similarly emphasizes that robust cybersecurity frameworks for UAV and UAM operations must include strong authentication mechanisms, encryption protocols, and intrusion detection systems to ensure the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of communication links (). Maintaining system integrity and preventing cyber intrusions are essential for the safe deployment. Additionally, concerns about signal interception were also discussed. Because UAVs rely extensively on wireless data links for navigation, telemetry, and command and control, these communication channels represent a critical attack surface where adversaries may conduct eavesdropping, jamming, spoofing, or denial-of-service attacks, threatening both operational safety and data security (; ).

> “That matter of cyber security, you know, I certainly won’t name names, but I distinctly remember being at an industry round table dinner and asking one of the OEMs what the discussion surrounding cyber security, you know, how the progress and this was a couple of years back, how the progress was coming along, and their answer was a bit startling to me. > “The problem of signal interception is severe. An attacker could compromise the aircraft without the operator noticing until it's too late if they can duplicate control signals.”

> “We know with the drones that that is a huge risk that can happen. So a lot of attention must be put on this. It’s a key priority.”

As operations become increasingly dependent on digital systems, the risk of cyberattacks—such as GPS interference and remote hacking of flight control systems—grows significantly. Research on unmanned aerial systems consistently identifies Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) interference, including jamming and spoofing, as one of the most critical cybersecurity threats because these systems rely heavily on satellite-based positioning, navigation, and timing data for autonomous or remotely piloted flight operations (; ). Experts note that even traditional air navigation service providers (ANSPs) are still learning to effectively address cybersecurity threats. This challenge is reflected in broader infrastructure studies showing that GNSS-dependent systems across transportation, telecommunications, and other critical sectors remain vulnerable to intentional radio-frequency interference and spoofing attacks capable of manipulating positioning or timing information (). The rise of drones and automated flight systems introduces new vulnerabilities that require robust security measures. Because unmanned vehicles depend on interconnected sensors, software, and communication networks to execute automated tasks, weaknesses in these cyber-physical systems can allow malicious actors to intercept data streams, manipulate control signals, or disrupt vehicle operations (). To safeguard remote connectivity, it is essential to implement strong encryption, continuous monitoring, and rapid response protocols. Cybersecurity is a technical concern and crucial for maintaining public trust in autonomous flight operations. Cybersecurity frameworks proposed in the UAV literature therefore emphasize encryption of communication links, authentication mechanisms, redundancy through additional sensors, and anomaly detection techniques to identify spoofed or manipulated navigation signals (; ).

> “That’s the feedback I receive from everyone in the industry, and also states too, for aviation in general and this new industry, drones, in particular. But we know that security is not only safety, but security will be key, to make this industry successful, and yeah believe me, I believe all the organizations, you know, in the aviation sector, they are still learning.”

Experts emphasized that reliable, low-latency communication is essential for remote pilots and autonomous systems, especially during emergencies. A brief 2-s delay could hinder a pilot's response and increase risk. Studies on remotely piloted aircraft systems demonstrate that communication latency and signal availability directly affect mission success and flight stability, with even moderate delays degrading system performance and reducing operational safety margins (). Although current communication latency is in milliseconds, any glitch or interruption could slow reactions and threaten safety. Simulation-based research on command-and-control (C2) links has shown that latency variability and communication loss can rapidly deteriorate aircraft performance during waypoint tracking and other mission-critical tasks, emphasizing the importance of maintaining consistent and predictable communication links (). Without advancements in fast, accurate communication, the widespread adoption of UAM/AAM remains uncertain. Ultra-reliable and low-latency communication (uRLLC) frameworks, often targeting latencies on the order of milliseconds and extremely low packet-loss probabilities, are therefore considered a foundational requirement for future autonomous aviation and intelligent transportation systems involving UAV operations ().

> “Reporting aircraft positions needs to be extremely precise and latency-free. Aircraft, this is where I want you to be, the pilot must say, and the aircraft must respond, Yes, this is where I am, and I am following instructions. Unless an RTK (Real-Time Kinematic) mechanism is included, the majority of our GPS is insufficiently accurate.”

Robust communication networks are needed to support both crewed and uncrewed operations. Experts caution against rushing into a specific technology choice, such as 5G or satellite communication, without clearly defining operational requirements. In safety-critical avionics systems, communication architectures must be designed to guarantee predictability, reliability, and fault tolerance, since flight control systems rely on distributed networks that connect sensors, actuators, and flight computers through communication protocols capable of maintaining deterministic timing behavior (). It is important to distinguish between safety-critical and non-safety-critical communications. Safety-critical systems—like air traffic control and vehicle command—must meet high reliability standards to function effectively even if the network fails. Research on UAV system reliability emphasizes that mission-critical applications require dependable communication subsystems linking the aircraft, ground control station, and onboard sensors, as any communication outage or data corruption can lead to mission failure or system loss (). Conversely, non-safety-critical services, like passenger connectivity, can utilize more adaptable infrastructure like IoT networks. Furthermore, redundancy and resilience are crucial. Since UAM depends on digital communication, ground-based systems must be resilient enough to maintain connectivity even if satellite navigation is disrupted. Because UAV systems operate as interconnected cyber-physical networks, dependability research highlights the importance of redundancy, fault tolerance, and network resilience to mitigate risks such as communication breakdowns, hardware failures, and environmental disturbances (). Autonomous operations could be jeopardized without reliable links, raising the risk of mid-air conflicts or loss of control. In distributed UAV systems or swarm operations, reliable communication becomes even more critical because coordination, task allocation, and computation sharing depend on timely data exchange between vehicles and control nodes ().

> “There is a clear right now tendency to and navigation in general to rely on space-based CNS, but at the same time we are seeing right now, and it's just, you know, for all these crises that we have around the world, that we we need to, how to maintain also, redundancy and a robust ground-based, and that that for me is is a challenge, ….it makes sense that we're relying more on space-based CNS, but at the same time ….how can we ensure continuity and resilience of the operations?”

Research on future UAS integration highlights that scalable operations depend on continuous digital connectivity and shared operational data across distributed stakeholders, enabling coordinated decision-making and automated separation management in increasingly complex airspace environments (). Experts referenced a past aviation accident in Washington, D.C., to underscore this need. In UAM and AAM operations, where aircraft are expected to operate autonomously or remotely, real-time data sharing among pilots, air traffic controllers, and vertiport managers will be essential. Studies on UTM similarly emphasize that safe integration of unmanned aircraft requires real-time exchange of surveillance, position, and intent data across the entire operational ecosystem to support conflict detection and traffic coordination (). Additionally, experts strongly advocate for integrating ground-based radar surveillance into remote pilot displays and vertiport management systems. Making this data accessible would enhance situational awareness and enable operators to make better-informed real-time decisions. Digital flight and advanced airspace management concepts proposed by NASA also rely on shared digital flight information and cooperative data exchange between operators and automated systems to ensure safe separation and coordinated flight path management (). Experts emphasized that, given current connectivity technologies, there is no reason for information sharing to remain fragmented. Recent research on drone data ecosystems further indicates that collaborative drone operations rely on real-time data exchange between aircraft, ground stations, and cloud-based infrastructures, enabling coordinated sensing, navigation, and mission execution across multiple stakeholders ().

### 4.4. Vertiport infrastructure and operational efficiency

Experts emphasized the importance of comprehensive planning for vertiports’ physical and digital infrastructure. Existing research similarly highlights that vertiports represent a critical component of the Urban Air Mobility ecosystem, acting as the interface between aerial and ground transportation systems and therefore requiring integrated planning that accounts for infrastructure design, airspace coordination, and passenger flow management (). A significant regulatory update involves consolidating EB-105A into a single advisory circular governing all powered-lift operations. The future design of vertiports remains uncertain—they may resemble multi-pad heliports or require entirely new configurations. This uncertainty reflects broader industry challenges, as vertiport infrastructure is still evolving and current design standards are largely adapted from heliport guidelines that may not fully support the high-frequency operations anticipated for Advanced Air Mobility systems (). While progress has been made in physical infrastructure, digital systems vital for efficient operations still need further development. Scholars have similarly noted that while conceptual vertiport layouts and infrastructure proposals exist, the operational frameworks governing scheduling, traffic flow, and real-time coordination remain underdeveloped (). Experts also expressed concerns about the lack of clear benchmarks, emphasizing the need to adopt existing best practices rather than develop entirely new systems.

> “Vertiports must be developed as fully integrated air mobility hubs, with real-time data sharing and operational management, rather than just landing strips.”

> “The question of whether vertiports will resemble multi-pad heliports or require an entirely distinct design is significant. Infrastructure developers are confused what to budget for in the absence of precise regulatory guidance.”

> “We are seeing progress on the physical infrastructure of vertiports, but the digital architecture, which governs how these technologies interact with air traffic control and operational management, requires more work.”

Experts believe there is a need for a structured reservation system to manage vertiport capacity efficiently. Unlike traditional airports with clearly defined approach paths, separation standards, and air traffic control overseeing landing sequences, vertiports will depend on automated systems for their high-frequency operations, which are currently missing. Recent simulation-based research on AAM infrastructure supports this concern, demonstrating that high-frequency vertiport operations require coordinated scheduling mechanisms and operational sequencing to avoid congestion, delays, and inefficient use of limited landing pads (). Since eVTOLs have limited hover times, pilots must reserve landing slots in advance to avoid delays and airborne congestion. Vertiport capacity studies further emphasize that infrastructure constraints such as turnaround time, pad availability, charging requirements, and emergency delays significantly influence throughput and must be considered when designing operational scheduling systems (). Vertiports risk bottlenecks, inefficiencies, and safety issues without such a system. Prioritizing emergency flights, such as medical transports or organ deliveries, was discussed. Creating clear traffic management protocols is crucial to ensure demand does not exceed capacity.

Experts agree that aircraft needing to land—especially during emergencies—should always receive priority over aircraft waiting to take off. A centralized vertiport management system should streamline this decision-making process, ensuring that landing pads are allocated efficiently. This requirement aligns with broader recommendations in the AAM literature emphasizing integrated traffic management systems that dynamically coordinate airspace operations, vertiport capacity, and flight sequencing to maintain safety and operational efficiency (). Additionally, operators must plan for scenarios where multiple aircraft encounter issues simultaneously, requiring contingency areas for several emergency landings. Communication with ATC will be crucial in managing these situations and ensuring vertiports are prepared to receive aircraft in distress. Integrating vertiports into existing airport environments further introduces additional coordination challenges with air traffic control systems, highlighting the need for new procedures and infrastructure designs to support safe sequencing between conventional aircraft and high-frequency eVTOL operations ().

> “ *An aircraft that needs a surface has precedence over an aircraft on the ground. So if an aircraft is not taxied into position even though they have a reservation, if you have a diversion, or you have, worse yet, an emergency, that aircraft takes precedence and can override the surface. Again, that can only happen if the surface is part of a common information system, right? Available to all the participants in the system.*”

> “Currently, airports have defined approach paths and separation regulations. Vertiports do not. That's a major gap that needs to be filled before we can safely grow operations.”

### 4.5. Regulatory harmonization and standardization

Experts highlight the importance of implementing comprehensive, data-driven standards for regulating detect-and-avoid systems, vertiport management, and airspace operations. A significant challenge is the absence of harmonized international standards. While the FAA is working on regulatory frameworks, other countries are developing their own, which could result in conflicting requirements. Participants emphasize the need for global cooperation to ensure interoperability across regions. This observation is strongly supported in the literature, which highlights that the rapid growth of unmanned aviation technologies has outpaced the development of coordinated regulatory frameworks across jurisdictions, creating fragmented oversight environments and inconsistent operational requirements between countries (; ). Such fragmentation complicates cross-border operations and slows technological adoption, particularly for systems intended to operate across multiple regulatory regimes (; ).

The International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) sets safety standards and promotes worldwide alignment on vital technologies. Experts insist that decisions regarding critical systems—such as communication protocols, navigation systems, and automation frameworks—should be made internationally rather than regionally. Without harmonization, operational costs may rise, efficiency could decline, and safety risks might increase, particularly for operators expanding into multiple markets. A unified global framework is essential for seamless interoperability across different regulatory jurisdictions. Research on unmanned aircraft integration similarly emphasizes that safe integration of UAVs into civil airspace requires harmonized safety requirements and coordinated certification processes involving multiple regulatory authorities, standards bodies, and operational stakeholders (). These studies highlight that interoperability across communications, navigation, surveillance, and air traffic management systems is critical for ensuring safe operations in shared national and international airspace ().

Four key standards—ASM, RTCA, IEEE, and SAE—still require full integration. Concerns were raised that some organizations are trying to set standards independently as the FAA forms regulations, which could lead to fragmentation. This might cause inefficiencies or result in merely recreating existing systems instead of creating a cohesive framework. One standard, SAE6968, still needs further development to meet industry needs. Uncertainty remains about how regulatory agencies will coordinate to ensure safe and efficient operations. Without unified, clear standards, the industry risks delays, safety issues, and slower adoption of new technologies. Studies of regulatory governance for emerging aerospace technologies similarly note that overlapping technical standards and competing certification frameworks can lead to regulatory duplication and inconsistent compliance requirements, ultimately increasing certification costs and delaying the deployment of innovative aviation technologies ().

> “When I’m thinking about the industry and all these companies …. we cannot have very different approaches in different regions. They will increase the cost. There will be a lot of, even, safety implications. So, for me, yes, the role of ICAO here is very important.”

Concerns exist regarding the slow pace of regulatory adaptation, as innovation has outpaced the capacity of standards organizations, leading to uncertainty for manufacturers and operators. Long-term guidance and consistent standards from regulators are crucial to provide the industry with a clear compliance roadmap. Experts also highlighted the need for the FAA to update the regulatory framework to address unique challenges and existing regulatory gaps. A key issue is the unclear certification requirements, particularly propulsion methods, propeller design, and aircraft materials. Without explicit regulatory guidance, manufacturers and operators face difficulty meeting certification standards. FAA oversight research similarly indicates that emerging aircraft technologies, particularly those incorporating advanced automation, new propulsion architectures, and complex digital systems, require the development of new performance-based oversight methods and regulatory evaluation tools to ensure that safety oversight evolves alongside technological innovation ().

> “The FAA is striving to specify certification requirements for these new platforms, particularly in terms of propulsion technologies, propeller design, and automation."

> “The FAA needs to finalize the certification requirements for these platforms. They’re still using a traditional paradigm, which may not work for UAM and may make scaling operations prohibitively expensive.”

Experts expressed concerns about training standards for human operators overseeing automated systems and GPS signal integrity. As reliance on AI and automation grows, new training programs will be essential to ensure operators can effectively supervise these technologies and intervene when necessary. Additionally, a gap has been identified in spectrum integrity. GPS interference presents significant risks, yet there are no dedicated standards to secure the signals used in navigation. Without explicit protections, these systems remain vulnerable. Research on GNSS navigation integrity similarly emphasizes that safety-critical aviation operations require robust integrity monitoring mechanisms capable of detecting faulty signals and issuing timely warnings to users when navigation data becomes unreliable (; ). These integrity monitoring systems, including fault detection and exclusion algorithms and protection-level calculations, are essential for maintaining trust in satellite navigation systems used for aviation guidance and automated flight operations (; ).

> “Automation will be critical in certification, but there is still dispute over how much human control is necessary. Without unambiguous FAA instructions, producers are left in the dark.”

Finally, experts highlighted that Beyond Visual Line of Sight (BVLOS) operations are recognized as vital; however, there is currently no established regulatory framework. Without a clear position from the FAA, progress may be delayed, leaving the industry without a definitive path to full deployment. Experts also point out the lack of standardized guidelines for traffic separation and right-of-way rules. Unlike traditional aviation with well-defined separation standards, UAM aircraft will operate in complex, low-altitude environments with higher traffic densities. BVLOS regulatory research demonstrates that current UAS regulations, such as FAA Part 107, primarily support visual line-of-sight operations and therefore limit the scalability of advanced UAS services, including long-range inspections, cargo delivery, and emergency response missions. The safe implementation of BVLOS operations requires robust communication links, detect-and-avoid systems, operator training frameworks, and clearly defined operational standards to maintain safety in shared airspace (; ).

> “One of the most significant issues is the absence of separation criteria for UAM aircraft. Commercial aviation currently has well-established separation rules; however, UAM operations do not.”

### 4.6. Contingency planning and safety

Unmanned aircraft introduce new failure modes not encountered in traditional crewed aviation. Existing aircraft systems are certified, assuming a pilot is onboard to monitor and respond to issues. However, UAM and AAM will require automated or remote solutions for emergency management. Without a pilot to take corrective action, systems must be designed with fail-safes to ensure controlled emergency responses. Experts emphasized the importance of strong system redundancy, especially in critical flight functions. Operating at the edge of computing capacity was also highlighted as essential. Aircraft need to make autonomous, real-time decisions. Unmanned operations could present significant risks without these safeguards, making addressing these unique failure modes crucial before widespread deployment. This concern is consistent with the autonomous systems literature, which emphasizes that autonomous platforms introduce novel failure modes because monitoring, diagnosis, and recovery functions are redistributed across software, sensors, communication links, and remote human operators rather than a co-located pilot (; ). Research on human reliability in autonomous systems similarly notes that increasing levels of autonomy do not remove human involvement, but instead shift humans into supervisory and recovery roles, creating new pathways to failure if contingency architectures and operator support are insufficient (). In addition, resilience engineering studies in multi-UAV systems and system-of-systems contexts show that mission success under disruption depends on architecting resilience explicitly into communications, control, path planning, and resource allocation rather than assuming it will emerge automatically from autonomy alone (;, ).

> “One major concern is redundancy. Without a pilot to take remedial action, systems must have fail-safes to ensure continuing operation or a controlled emergency response.”

The literature strongly supports this concern, emphasizing that redundancy, fault tolerance, and reconfiguration are foundational to safe autonomous operations. Studies of dependable autonomous systems distinguish robustness to environmental uncertainty from fault tolerance to failures in system resources and argue that autonomous systems require both, especially when decisional mechanisms are safety-critical (). More broadly, resilience-driven engineering research shows that redundancy should not be viewed only as duplicated hardware, but also as monitoring, prognostics, adaptive control, and alternative functional pathways that preserve safe performance during disruptions (). Recent multi-UAV resilience work further demonstrates that recoverability depends not only on component survival but also on the ability of the formation or network to reconfigure after damage while preserving mission coverage and communication integrity ().

Experts noted that emergency procedures are still being developed, especially regarding how emergencies affect nearby aircraft. Unlike traditional aviation, where pilots can coordinate and respond in real-time, UAM systems must account for automated responses and remote decision-making. Experts believe many proposed vertiport sites lack dedicated fire and emergency response equipment, risking slow incident response. There are also concerns about how emergency responders can efficiently reach accident sites, especially in urban areas where vertiports might be on rooftops or in densely populated neighborhoods. The discussion also highlighted the challenges posed by lithium battery fires, which require specialized response strategies different from those used for conventional aviation fuel fires. Unexpected events could cause disruptions in airspace operations if clear emergency protocols are not in place. Developing and implementing comprehensive emergency response plans is essential. These observations align with sociotechnical safety research showing that failures in autonomous systems are rarely purely technical and instead emerge from interactions between technology, operations, organizations, and emergency management structures (). Research on disaster-response systems similarly finds that resilience depends on the availability of well-managed redundancy, cross-functional coordination, and contingency procedures that can absorb disruption without allowing local failures to escalate into broader system breakdowns (). In urban and hazardous-environment UAV applications, researchers have likewise emphasized that UAV systems are especially valuable in dangerous or inaccessible areas, but only when supported by coordinated sensing, communications, and response infrastructures capable of operating under degraded conditions (; ).

> “Fire response at vertiports is a big issue. Many proposed vertiport sites lack specific emergency resources.”

Another key concern was the loss of communication links between remote pilots and aircraft. Automated systems must be capable of responding to this scenario by executing pre-programmed emergency maneuvers. Experts rejected that losing a control link should result in an uncontrolled flight, arguing for redundant communication systems that allow pilots to re-establish contact or guide aircraft to designated safe zones. This is one of the clearest themes in the UAS contingency-management literature. identify lost link as a contingency unique to unmanned aircraft that requires some degree of autonomous contingency operation because remote pilots may face communication latency and reduced situational awareness during emergencies. Building on this, automated contingency management research argues that onboard systems should be able to assist or replace the pilot-in-command when necessary, especially during C2 link loss, by selecting predictable, risk-minimizing responses that preserve an equivalent level of safety to crewed aviation (). Studies of lost-link planning also emphasize that contingency procedures should be standardized, automated where possible, and designed to maximize predictability for air traffic control and other airspace users (; ). MITRE concept work similarly notes that controllers currently have limited situational awareness of unmanned aircraft contingency trajectories unless those trajectories are stored, retrievable, and digitally processable within ATM systems (). NASA operational best-practices research further recommends standardized lost-link procedures and mitigation strategies that minimize impacts on other NAS users and support safe continuation, diversion, or termination of flight as appropriate ().

> *“I think it is absolutely necessary to plan for this contingency. There has to be some alternative communication link such that or there has to be some capability such that aircraft can be capable of handling basic navigation even in the case of link loss. should handle detect and avoid should be able to conduct the DIA maneuver even if the link is lost with the ground station. And of course it has to know to return the mission and maybe to return to the base or something like that so that it doesn’t get lost. …. I guess the one way of handling it would be having some sort of redundant link through which you can communicate to that aircraft that lost the primary control link*.”

Satellite-based navigation provides high accuracy but remains vulnerable to GPS interference. Experts recommended a hybrid approach—combining space-based navigation with ground-based enhancements—to help mitigate these risks. There was also discussion about “graceful degradation” strategies, where aircraft could rely on secondary systems if a primary navigation failure occurs. These backup mechanisms will be essential in highly automated systems where human intervention may not always be possible. This is consistent with research on dynamic safety management and autonomic safety frameworks for autonomous systems, which argues that systems operating in open environments cannot rely solely on static worst-case assumptions and must instead monitor their own condition and context at runtime, switching to safer configurations when needed (; ). Graceful degradation in this context refers not simply to reduced performance, but to preserving safety through monitored fallback modes, alternative sensing or control pathways, and controlled adaptation when primary capabilities are lost. Multi-UAV resilience research similarly shows that recoverability depends on maintaining both communication and mission performance under disturbance, often through reconfiguration of formation, task allocation, or network structure (; ). In system-of-systems terms, this is closely related to stand-in redundancy, where remaining elements are re-tasked to compensate for degraded or failed components rather than relying only on one-to-one backups ().

> “If we think about this, highly automated ecosystem, in the moment \[where\], ‘yes, there is a disruption.’ How \[do\] you manage that? And that that will be a key challenge because the human will not be able to take over. So you will need to think … different type of approaches to manage this technical failures ….like graceful degradation, things like that.”

While DAA systems can mitigate mid-air conflicts, they are not designed to handle unpredictable elements such as bird activity. Experts suggested that additional technologies, such as radar-based bird detection systems, may be necessary to minimize these risks. This limitation is consistent with broader UAV perception research showing that environmental perception remains difficult in unstructured, cluttered, and dynamic operating environments. Studies on autonomous obstacle avoidance note that uncertain obstacles and environmental clutter pose significant collision risks and that reliable avoidance requires richer environmental perception, multi-sensor fusion, and classification methods capable of distinguishing meaningful threats from irrelevant objects or noise (). Related work in environmental and remote-sensing UAV operations also highlights that hazardous environments, poor weather, fragile platforms, limited onboard power, and sensing constraints all reduce the reliability of perception and safe flight performance (; ). These findings support expert concerns that DAA alone is insufficient for handling all real-world hazards and that complementary sensing and filtering approaches are needed.

> “ *I think ground clutter and some flying objects like birds or I don’t know bats, for example. Those are problems for radar especially because they give a lot of false alarms. So I guess some advanced filtering algorithms and machine learning techniques could be used to distinguish between actual meant traffic and the true threats rather than establish tracking these false alarms caused by ground clutter and airborne obstacles*.”

Achieving accurate landings in densely built urban areas is a significant challenge due to the likelihood of obstacles surrounding vertiports. Experts recommend visual guidance systems, such as fiducial markers similar to QR codes, to improve landing accuracy. These markers can provide highly accurate positional data, potentially surpassing traditional Instrument Landing Systems (ILS) or GPS-based methods. As operations expand, reliable landing capabilities will become even more essential. Automated missed approach procedures are also vital for safety, given eVTOLs’ limited energy reserves. Aircraft should be able to assess fuel levels in real time and decide whether to attempt another landing, divert to an alternative vertiport, or return to the starting point without human input. Unlike traditional aircraft, eVTOLs have limited electrical energy reserves. Experts advise that airspace design include energy management strategies and establish contingency landing zones for safe diversions. They also emphasize the importance of adaptive flight planning to address these challenges. These recommendations align closely with automated contingency management research, which emphasizes that contingency responses should be embedded into mission planning through predefined reaction catalogs, alternative routes, and designated landing sites so that the aircraft remains predictable even during abnormal operations (; ). Research on contingency trajectories also shows that predictability for controllers and other airspace users improves when unmanned aircraft have predefined and digitally shareable emergency trajectories rather than ad hoc pilot improvisation (). More generally, resilient system design literature argues that adaptive planning under resource constraints is a core requirement for resilient operation, especially when continued safe performance depends on real-time balancing of mission objectives against energy, health state, and environmental risk (; ).

> “We require pre-defined emergency landing zones in metropolitan areas. If something goes wrong, where does the plane go?”

Experts noted that micro-weather conditions, such as localized wind shifts, turbulence, and sudden visibility changes, can significantly impact landing accuracy. To address this, vertiports will need advanced weather-monitoring infrastructure to provide real-time updates to incoming aircraft. This concern is supported by both UAS operations and vertiport literature. Work on hazardous-environment UAV sensing emphasizes that valuable UAV applications often occur precisely in conditions characterized by uncertainty, dangerous terrain, and rapidly changing weather, making real-time environmental sensing critical for safe operation (). Environmental UAV studies likewise identify poor weather and wind as among the most persistent operational barriers affecting reliability and safe deployment (). In the UAM context, recent vertiport and infrastructure studies also stress that vertiports must function as integrated operational nodes rather than simple landing pads, with throughput, safety, and scalability depending on real-time environmental data, adaptive zoning, and coordinated operational management (; ). User-centred vertiport network research similarly emphasizes that real-time information sharing and operational integration are central to safe and publicly acceptable UAM implementation ().

> “I really see a new role for, you know, the vertiport arrival and departure management because you will need more information about micro weather around these locations. … We will need to have more accurate information about all the infrastructure around the landing sites. So we will need to, again, be able to manage far more information and use that information to automate all these operations, the arrivals and departures, because again the performance of these vehicles are different.”

### 4.7. Additional identified challenges

#### 4.7.1. Business model & economic viability

Experts questioned the long-term profitability of UAM and AAM. Although technological progress is rapid, the business models remain uncertain, especially concerning infrastructure costs, regulatory compliance, and operational expenses. Several studies examining the economic feasibility of urban air mobility similarly emphasize that large-scale implementation requires significant capital investment in vertiports, charging infrastructure, aircraft fleets, and air traffic management systems, creating substantial financial uncertainty during early deployment stages (;; ). Experts pointed out that heliports have historically struggled to generate steady revenue, which raises concerns that vertiports may face similar financial hurdles. Historical analyses of helicopter-based urban air services highlight that high operating costs, limited demand, and community opposition contributed to the decline of many helicopter commuter services in the mid-20th century, raising similar questions about the long-term sustainability of future air taxi networks if cost structures remain high (). Consequently, they emphasized the importance of developing a clear funding strategy. Expanding operations could prove difficult without a well-defined plan for covering costs related to infrastructure development, maintenance, and operations. Vehicle design and cost modeling studies also indicate that aircraft acquisition costs, battery performance limitations, and operational utilization rates significantly affect the overall economics of UAM systems and the price passengers would need to pay for services (). Economic viability will hinge on balancing affordability for users with the need for operators to maintain profitable, sustainable businesses over the long term. Demand modeling research further suggests that passenger willingness to adopt UAM services is strongly influenced by travel cost, reliability, and time savings compared to ground transportation alternatives, indicating that pricing strategies will play a crucial role in determining market success ().

> “Vertiports and heliports have typically struggled for profitability. What leads us to believe UAM will be different? The business model is still unclear.”

> “We need a clear finance strategy. Infrastructure, maintenance, and operational costs must be funded in a way that balances affordability and profitability.”

#### 4.7.2. Uncrewed aerial systems & passenger transport

Experts discussed whether passengers should be allowed on uncrewed aerial systems, highlighting the significant risks. Passenger flights add more complexity compared to cargo transport. Full reliance on remote control and automation is essential without an onboard pilot. Research on the evolution of advanced air mobility operations indicates that most proposed deployment strategies foresee a gradual transition from piloted aircraft toward remotely supervised and eventually fully autonomous passenger systems (). Experts emphasized the need for clear regulations and technical solutions before uncrewed passenger services can become feasible. Studies focusing on autonomous aircraft development emphasize that reliable sensor fusion, flight control algorithms, and communication networks are required to ensure safe operations in dense urban environments where aircraft must interact with complex airspace and ground infrastructure (). Addressing these issues is crucial for achieving safety and public trust comparable to traditional aviation. Full autonomy in passenger transport remains a major challenge without strong security and operational safeguards. Behavioral research on emerging transport technologies further demonstrates that trust in automation and perceived safety represent key determinants influencing whether individuals are willing to use autonomous mobility systems, including autonomous aerial vehicles ().

> “We are not yet ready for passenger transit. Cargo operations are great, but flying people without a pilot requires complete trust in remote control systems and automatic safety procedures.”

> “Public trust is a major concern. Would you board an uncrewed flight today? Most people wouldn’t, which is a huge barrier to adoption.”

#### 4.7.3. Community engagement & public acceptance

Experts highlighted the importance of engaging communities early to gain public support and acceptance. Local resistance could pose a significant obstacle to widespread adoption without early outreach and education. Research examining public perception of urban air mobility identifies safety concerns, noise exposure, privacy risks, and visual intrusion as some of the most important factors shaping public acceptance of aerial mobility systems in urban environments (). Participants emphasized involving communities in assessing the feasibility of air corridors that benefit passenger and cargo transport. Concerns about noise, safety, and urban impact will also influence public opinion. Behavioral adoption studies further indicate that perceived usefulness, travel time savings, and trust in automated technologies significantly influence individuals’ willingness to adopt UAM services (). Building trust requires transparent communication about safety protocols, regulations, and the advantages of UAM and AAM. Addressing public opposition through ongoing engagement is crucial, as delays could hinder progress. Research on technology acceptance models applied to UAM similarly shows that social attitudes, familiarity with automation, and perceived reliability strongly influence adoption intentions among potential users ().

#### 4.7.4. Training & workforce development

Experts noted that training programs are not yet widely discussed or established. There is no clear framework detailing the necessary training for remote pilots, maintenance teams, and other key system operators. Existing research highlights that although technological development of UAM platforms has progressed rapidly, comparatively little work has addressed pilot education, training frameworks, and operational qualification standards for these systems, leaving a major gap in workforce preparation for future operations (). Concerns have been raised about the number of remote aircraft one operator can safely manage simultaneously. Human factors research on emerging UAM operations suggests that future systems may involve new operational roles, such as remote pilots or system supervisors overseeing multiple aircraft, which raises unresolved questions regarding workload, monitoring demands, and cognitive limitations (). Additionally, there is uncertainty regarding effective training methods for personnel in these roles. Scholars have emphasized that UAM operations will require new training paradigms integrating aviation knowledge, automation management, and human-system interaction skills, as traditional pilot training models may not adequately address highly automated or remotely supervised flight operations (). The industry must also prepare for emerging technologies like autonomous systems and advanced air traffic management tools. Urban Air Mobility ecosystems depend on the integration of advanced automation, electrified propulsion systems, and new traffic management architectures, which will fundamentally change operational roles and competencies required from aviation personnel (). Without a well-defined and structured workforce training strategy, the safety and efficiency of operations could be compromised. Researchers have therefore identified workforce development and training standardization as critical enablers for safe UAM deployment, alongside infrastructure, regulatory frameworks, and technological maturation (). Therefore, developing comprehensive training programs should be a priority for ongoing research and regulation.

> “There is no established framework for educating remote pilots, maintenance technicians, and other critical operators in the UAM ecosystem. That’s a significant gap that must be addressed.”

> “How many aircraft can a remote pilot operate at once? The industry has yet to establish clear guidelines on this.”

#### 4.7.5. Overlooked challenges

Experts pointed out several overlooked challenges in integrating UAM and AAM into the transportation system. A concern is the “last mile” charging issue—transferring electric aircraft from the landing site to the charging station without depleting their batteries through hover taxiing. Energy management and charging logistics have been identified as key operational constraints for eVTOL systems, as battery capacity, charging rates, and infrastructure availability directly affect mission feasibility and turnaround times (). Developing efficient ground movement processes will be essential to minimize energy waste. Studies on vertiport energy demand show that the charging requirements of eVTOL fleets are strongly influenced by mission schedules, aircraft turnaround times, and infrastructure layout, highlighting the need for integrated energy-management strategies in UAM networks (). Additionally, insurance presents another potential obstacle. Securing affordable, comprehensive coverage will be critical for operators to successfully test and deploy new technologies. Previous market analyses of UAM implementation have emphasized that regulatory uncertainty, certification requirements, and risk perception among stakeholders, including insurers, may slow adoption if clear operational standards are not established early in the industry's development (). Insurers may be reluctant to provide coverage without established industry standards and regulatory frameworks, leading to financial and operational uncertainties. These broader institutional and infrastructural barriers, including regulatory approval, infrastructure readiness, and economic viability, have been widely identified as major challenges that must be addressed before UAM systems can scale to routine commercial operations ().

## 5\. General discussion

Across all four groups of interviews, a range of technical, regulatory, and operational challenges were identified in the development and deployment of UAM and AAM. While each group focused on different aspects, several recurring themes emerged, including the need for advanced DAA systems, improvements in airspace management, enhanced automation, regulatory harmonization, robust cybersecurity, and vertiport infrastructure planning. These discussions underscored the need to balance automation with human oversight, while ensuring that regulatory and technological frameworks evolve to support the safe integration of emerging aviation technologies. Expert interviews allowed for this insight, which was not previously captured. The triangulation of expert perspectives with existing academic literature further reinforces many of the challenges identified through the interviews. Across multiple domains, including airspace integration, autonomy, workforce development, infrastructure planning, and public acceptance, recent studies consistently highlight similar barriers to implementation. Research on the broader UAM ecosystem indicates that successful deployment depends not only on technological readiness but also on coordinated regulatory frameworks, integrated digital infrastructure, and effective human-system collaboration strategies (;; ). Additionally, several reviews of the UAM ecosystem emphasize that economic viability, public acceptance, and workforce readiness represent key determinants of long-term scalability (; ). These findings support the expert observations presented in this study and suggest that the barriers identified in the interviews reflect broader systemic challenges across the emerging AAM ecosystem rather than isolated technical limitations.

A major concern among the groups was ensuring the development and integration of dependable DAA systems. While significant progress has been made, gaps remain in low-altitude airspace coverage, particularly for non-cooperative aircraft. Experts stressed that DAA must go beyond providing situational awareness and actively enable automated conflict resolution, reducing the reliance on remote pilots for split-second decision-making. A shift toward automated traffic sequencing and 4D trajectory management was suggested as a necessary step for managing airspace congestion efficiently. While full autonomy is still a future goal, the current focus is on assisting remote pilots with automated systems that help with navigation, airspace deconfliction, and collision avoidance. However, concerns were raised about relying too much on AI without enough human oversight, especially in emergencies where human intuition and adaptability are still essential. Another critical implication emerging from the triangulated findings concerns workforce preparation for increasingly automated aviation environments. Recent research on human factors in UAM operations suggests that the transition from conventional piloted aircraft to remotely supervised or highly automated aircraft will significantly change operator roles and required competencies (). Instead of traditional pilot skill sets alone, operators will likely require hybrid capabilities combining aviation knowledge with automation monitoring, systems supervision, and data-driven decision making. Workforce development analyses further suggest that the UAM ecosystem will require a broader range of specialized roles, including vertiport operations personnel, fleet supervision managers, UTM system operators, and cybersecurity specialists responsible for protecting digital aviation infrastructure (; ). Without coordinated education and certification pathways for these emerging roles, workforce shortages and inconsistent training standards could become a significant barrier to scaling operations.

The need for real-time communication and data-sharing networks was another recurring theme. A common operating picture must be accessible to all stakeholders, including air traffic controllers, remote pilots, and vertiport managers. However, the absence of standardized communication protocols and concerns about network reliability present significant challenges. Redundancy in communication and ensuring secure and encrypted data transmission, coupled with real-time anomaly detection systems, was recommended as a priority for the industry. Aircraft must be capable of autonomous emergency actions, such as diverting to a specific landing site, executing a missed approach during an operational failure, and ensuring sufficient fuel and battery reserves to handle unexpected delays or diversions. Infrastructure readiness represents another key area where expert perspectives align closely with existing research. Studies on vertiport design emphasize that these facilities must function as integrated mobility hubs rather than simple landing pads, incorporating passenger processing systems, energy infrastructure, digital traffic management systems, and connections to existing transportation networks (; ). In particular, energy supply requirements for electric vertical take-off and landing aircraft may place substantial demands on urban electrical grids, especially in high-traffic metropolitan vertiports. Efficient ground operations, charging logistics, and energy management strategies will therefore be critical to maintaining operational efficiency and preventing infrastructure bottlenecks. These findings further reinforce the importance of coordinated planning among aviation regulators, urban planners, energy providers, and transportation authorities when developing future UAM infrastructure.

All identified themes in this research are linked to regulation. While multiple organizations are working on competing standards, no unified framework exists for certifying technologies. Experts urged greater coordination among regulatory bodies such as the FAA, ICAO, and other international agencies to ensure that standards and certification are harmonized globally. Without this alignment, the industry will face inefficiencies, higher costs, and delays in adoption. Economic sustainability also emerged as an important consideration across both expert interviews and the supporting literature. Several studies examining UAM market feasibility note that although technological progress in eVTOL aircraft has accelerated, long-term economic viability remains uncertain due to high infrastructure costs, certification expenses, and operational constraints associated with dense urban environments (; ). Vertiport construction, energy infrastructure, and digital airspace management systems represent substantial capital investments that must be balanced against uncertain passenger demand and regulatory timelines. Historical comparisons with heliport operations further illustrate the risk that infrastructure investments may struggle to achieve profitability without sufficient traffic volume and integration with broader transportation networks. These findings suggest that technological readiness alone will not determine the success of UAM systems; sustainable business models and coordinated public-private funding strategies will likely be essential for long-term adoption.

Identified expert themes remained consistent as different groups examined various UAM/AAM lifecycle stages. This consistency shows that these topics are crucial from early operations to mature phases. The recurring themes across different groups emphasize the connection of key issues throughout the entire spectrum of operations. Additionally, the overlap in themes among participants with different backgrounds highlights the importance of these issues for all stakeholders involved. Public acceptance also represents a critical factor influencing the long-term viability of UAM systems. Research examining societal perceptions of autonomous aerial transport consistently shows that trust in automation, perceived safety, noise concerns, and privacy considerations strongly influence public willingness to adopt these services (; ). Surveys indicate that while many individuals may initially accept piloted eVTOL aircraft, acceptance decreases significantly for fully autonomous passenger flights without a human operator onboard. These findings align with the concerns expressed by experts in this study regarding the challenges of deploying uncrewed passenger transport. Early operational strategies may therefore need to prioritize cargo transport or piloted passenger operations while gradually building public familiarity and confidence in automated aviation technologies.

### 5.1. Practical implications

The expert interviews conducted for this study helped establish realistic expectations and clear mandates for successfully implementing UAM/AAM. Engaging with industry experts provides unique perspectives and insights into the significant challenges faced during deployment. The findings from this research can be further developed through collaboration with industry stakeholders and regulators to continue advancing the deployment of these technologies.

While many studies focus on key issues such as public acceptance, airspace management, regulation, safety, and cybersecurity, expert interviews with those actively developing AAM systems offer a valuable, experience-driven perspective. Experts understand firsthand the current challenges, including developmental progress and foreseeable hurdles. Insights facilitate in-depth discussions of both well-known and less-explored topics related to UAM/AAM, going beyond the typical research scope. Industry insiders can discuss the ongoing challenges and the steps required for full-scale deployment. The findings also highlight the growing importance of workforce preparation as UAM systems evolve toward higher levels of automation. Several studies emphasize that the emergence of remotely supervised and autonomous flight operations will require new educational programs and certification frameworks to prepare operators for these roles. Training will likely need to incorporate automation management, human-machine interaction, cybersecurity awareness, and digital airspace management competencies in addition to traditional aviation knowledge. These requirements suggest that universities, aviation training organizations, and industry partners will need to collaborate closely to develop standardized training pathways capable of supporting the emerging UAM workforce. For example, while DAA is commonly addressed in the literature, this research explores hierarchical control levels and the debate over aircraft separation authority and airspace sanitation, providing real-world insights beyond common research studies.

Furthermore, interviewees emphasized overlooked issues, including rooftop vertiport fire response strategies, employee training, workforce development, digital data-sharing ecosystems, specific AI algorithm needs, micro-weather conditions, radar-based bird detection systems, and critical vehicle-mounted cameras. While many studies highlight the lack of regulatory standards for deployment, these expert insights provide realistic details and specify which regulations need revision or development to ensure successful implementation. Including this human perspective in research adds valuable depth and nuance to the overall understanding. This study can help raise awareness of challenges to stimulate significant regulatory changes.

Across the three fields used to draw participants, there were both convergent and divergent perspectives on UAM/AAM. In general, academia is focused on the research-oriented aspects of the challenges to implementing UAM/AAM and, compared to industry and government, is more likely to consider the theoretical and conceptual aspects of implementation. Industry is quite focused on the practical aspects of deploying this technology and is very interested in scalability, efficiency, and deployment. Government stakeholders tend to be concentrated on certification, regulatory oversight, public safety, and standardized frameworks necessary for large-scale implementation.

While substantial agreement was found across the three fields, there were some differences in priorities based on the stakeholder field. Differences were most commonly found in how each field prioritized specific challenges and future needs. Those in academia were more likely to emphasize the long-term system resilience, theoretical frameworks for automation and safety, and trust calibration. Those in the industry field prioritized the near-term operational capabilities such as AI-assisted optimization, low-latency communications, and scalable remote piloting. Meanwhile, those in the government field saw concerns surrounding certification pathways, harmonized standards, liability, and oversight in increasingly automated environments. These points of convergence and divergence showcase the multidimensional nature of UAM/AAM integration and underscore the need for interdisciplinary cooperation among researchers, industry leaders, and regulatory agencies throughout the development and implementation process.

### 5.3. Recommendations for the development of UAM/AAM

#### 5.3.1. Short-term recommendations (0–5 years)

In the near term, efforts should prioritize establishing foundational regulatory frameworks, operational standards, and workforce preparation strategies (;; ). Regulatory agencies should focus on developing certification pathways for eVTOL aircraft, detect-and-avoid systems, and remote pilot operations (; ). At the same time, early workforce training programs should be developed in collaboration with universities, aviation training organizations, and industry partners to prepare personnel for emerging roles in automated aviation systems (; ). Pilot demonstration programs and limited-scale cargo operations may also provide valuable operational data while gradually building public trust in UAM technologies (;; ).

#### 5.3.2. Medium-term recommendations (5–10 years)

Over the medium term, the focus should shift toward scaling operational infrastructure and integrating advanced traffic management systems capable of supporting higher traffic densities (;; ). This includes expanding vertiport networks, implementing UTM integration with conventional air traffic management systems, and developing standardized communication and navigation architectures across regions (;; ). Regulatory harmonization among international aviation authorities will become increasingly important to ensure interoperability and prevent fragmented regulatory environments (;; ). Workforce development programs must also expand to address growing demand for specialized roles in fleet supervision, vertiport operations, and automated airspace management (;; ).

#### 5.3.3. Long-term recommendations (10+ years)

In the long term, the successful integration of UAM into the broader transportation ecosystem will depend on highly automated operations, resilient digital infrastructure, and widespread public acceptance (;; ). This phase may involve the gradual transition toward autonomous passenger operations supported by advanced AI-driven traffic management systems and integrated multimodal transportation networks (; ). Achieving this vision will require continued investment in safety assurance frameworks, cybersecurity protections, and resilient system architectures capable of operating reliably in complex urban environments (;; ). Long-term success will ultimately depend on sustained collaboration between regulators, industry stakeholders, researchers, and urban planners (;; ).

### 5.4. Limitations

A key strength of this research is its ability to identify real-world challenges and expert opinions that are not typically available in formal industry reports or regulatory discussions. However, like all qualitative studies, it has limitations. The findings reflect the interviewees' perspectives and may not encompass all industry challenges. The sample size for this research was intentionally small. Although efforts were made to include a diverse group of experts, the sample was necessarily more limited than in large-scale quantitative studies. Additionally, experts in this study were selected based on their expertise, which introduced some selection bias. Experts may also be influenced by their profession or organizational roles, further limiting the study's findings. Moreover, at the time of data collection, few experts had direct operational experience in real-world UAM operations. Expert responses are anticipatory rather than observational. As UAM/AAM rapidly progresses, expert opinions may change. Furthermore, this study used a cross-sectional approach, capturing participant responses at a single point in time. This further limits the ability to assess how expert opinions can change over time. Previous exposure to UAM/AAM information and training may also have affected responses. Finally, although qualitative coding procedures were employed in this study, no intercoder reliability measures were used, which affected the interpretation of responses.

Despite these limitations, the insights gathered provide a valuable foundation for further discussion and research. By engaging directly with those shaping the future of UAM and AAM, this study highlights technical and non-technical barriers that must be addressed for successful implementation.

## 6\. Conclusions

This qualitative study offers insights that add to the expanding literature on UAM and AAM by collecting expert opinions on practical implementation challenges. These findings extend prior research, which has primarily examined each challenge separately, without expert opinion. Recent meta-reviews provide valuable insights into existing UAM/AAM literature through secondary sources, highlighting retrospective challenges (). However, this research uniquely complements prior studies by synthesizing perspectives from diverse stakeholders and incorporating human expertise. These stakeholders include academia, industry, and regulatory and government bodies. Importantly, this study combines findings to show how challenges interact not only among stakeholders but also across different stages of UAM/AAM development. This highlights the importance of the interdependencies among systems and stakeholders for successful implementation.

This study identified clear expectations for future research and the necessary steps for the implementation of UAM/AAM. Longitudinal studies may be necessary to determine how views change as operational experience increases and system frameworks evolve. Future research should build on this study's findings and address operational aspects, including training, emergency response planning, public acceptance, and regulatory framework alignment, to support safe and scalable UAM/AAM integration.
